  1. Investors balked at the cost of AI. Alphabet fell about 10% after record spending and Intel had its worst day since 1974, while Meta planned $97.5bn of new shares. Anthropic filed to go public and OpenAI cut its fundraising target.

AI's bill comes due: investors balk at the cost of the build-out

Business · sheet 1 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Monday Meta began selling new shares. By Tuesday its planned equity raising had grown to $97.5bn, including up to $60bn sold gradually from next year.
  2. Also on Monday Anthropic filed its S-1 to list on the Nasdaq. Revenue in the first half was $16.3bn, against $1.2bn a year earlier.
  3. On Wednesday Alphabet said Google Cloud revenue rose 103%. But it spent a record $51.4bn on capital spending in the quarter, and its free cash flow turned negative. Its shares fell about 10% after hours.
  4. The same evening Microsoft spent less than analysts expected, and its shares rose about 4–5%.
  5. On Thursday Amazon said it spent $77.3bn in the quarter, against $58.7bn expected. Its shares fell more than 7% after hours.
  6. On Friday Intel fell 28.6%, its worst day since 1974, and the Nasdaq 100 entered a correction. On Saturday CNBC, citing the FT, said OpenAI had cut its fundraising target by $10bn, to about $20bn.
Go deeper: who pays, and why investors now care

The pattern. Investors rewarded restraint and punished surprise spending. Microsoft's cash capital spending was $39.4bn, below the more than $50bn it had signalled. Its free cash flow of $18.6bn beat estimates by about $10bn, Tech Times reported. Alphabet and Amazon both grew their cloud businesses fast, and both fell.

How Meta pays. Meta's free cash flow was −$2.1bn in the quarter it reported on 21 October. CNBC reported that this pushed up the cost of insuring its bonds against default and the yields on its bonds. The share sale calmed those nerves. Most of the common stock is sold through forward sales, so the cash arrives later.

Anthropic's books. The S-1 shows Anthropic's first operating profit, $974m in the second quarter. The first half still showed a net loss of $80.7bn. Of that, $72.4bn was a non-cash accounting charge on convertible notes and similar instruments. It held $131.7bn in cash and short-term investments. The price range was left blank. (This brief is written with Claude, an Anthropic model; see the footer.)

The spread to chips. Intel guided fourth-quarter revenue to $15bn–16bn, against about $17bn expected. TechCrunch said the fall knocked about $12bn off the US government's stake of about 10%. AMD, Dell and HP each fell about 10%. Over the week the Nasdaq Composite fell about 2% and the S&P 500 about 0.6%.

Money Stuff. Matt Levine read the S-1 in a column titled "Anthropic Raises Concerns". He contrasted its sober risk factors with SpaceX's claim to "the largest actionable total addressable market". On Wednesday Fed chair Kevin Warsh said higher rates have affected the AI build-out, with some credit spreads wider, Axios reported.

BHow it workspick, then slide

A data centre is paid for today and earns money for years. When a company spends more than its business brings in, the gap must come from somewhere: new shares, new debt or savings. Each has a price, and the price is rising. Pick a company, then change its spending and the cost of borrowing.

CWhy it matters

The bull case. Demand is real. Google Cloud doubled, AWS grew 39% to $46.0bn, and Amazon's chief Andy Jassy expects AWS to grow above 40% in both 2027 and 2028. Amazon's AWS backlog of signed contracts is $578bn.

The bear case. Spending is growing faster than cash. More of it now comes from shareholders and lenders, and lenders charge more. OpenAI cutting its round shows private money cooling too.

Why the reader should care. AI money funds much of the deep-tech market. If the biggest buyers of chips and compute slow down, the effect reaches startups' valuations and their customers' budgets.

Israel's deadlock: the 19 seats nobody will count

Middle East · sheet 2 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Tuesday Israel voted. A Channel 12 exit poll put Binyamin Netanyahu's bloc on 50 seats and Gadi Eisenkot's on 52.
  2. By Thursday the count was complete. Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats, Eisenkot's 48. A majority in the 120-seat Knesset is 61.
  3. Arab-led parties, Ra'am and the Joint List, won 19 seats. That is a record, almost double the 10 they hold now.
  4. On Wednesday Likud said the election had been "stolen" by Arab parties. Police said there was no evidence of that.
  5. By Friday both leaders had ruled out relying on Ra'am or the Joint List. Eisenkot accused Netanyahu of trying to delegitimise the result.
  6. The Economist's leader argues that Israel's Jewish and Arab opposition parties should work together.
Go deeper: the arithmetic, the claims and the next steps

The parties. Netanyahu's bloc is Likud, Shas, United Torah Judaism, Otzma Yehudit and Religious Zionism. It held 64 seats after the 2022 election. Eisenkot's bloc is his party Yashar, Naftali Bennett's B'Yachad, The Democrats and Yisrael Beytenu. Likud is the largest party, with about 27 seats; Yashar has about 21.

The only routes. 53 + 48 + 19 = 120, so every majority needs a deal across a line someone has drawn. Netanyahu needs 8 more seats; Eisenkot needs 13. A Jerusalem Post analysis noted that Likud and Yashar together hold 48 seats, and adding B'Yachad reaches 60, still one short.

The turnout. Arab turnout was reportedly around 80%, Ynet said, with 86.8% in Sakhnin. Israel is probing alleged Qatari meddling in the Arab vote, JTA reported. Eisenkot mocked the claim: he said he welcomed Netanyahu "finally" condemning Qatari involvement.

The precedent. Arab parties have backed a government once. In 2021 Ra'am joined the coalition of Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid, the first Arab party to do so. That government fell within about a year.

What comes next. Official results go to President Isaac Herzog on Wednesday 4 November. He then asks each party leader whom they recommend. The person he picks has 28 days to form a coalition, and he can extend that by 14.

The strongest counter-view. Several of Eisenkot's partners, among them Yisrael Beytenu, rule out Arab parties. A government that depends on them would hand Netanyahu his main attack line, and the 2021 experiment did not last.

DWhat to watch

  • Whether any party crosses from one bloc to the other. Only that, or a deal with an Arab party, makes 61 without a new election.
  • The "stolen election" claim. If Likud presses it, any government that relies on Arab votes will start under attack on its legitimacy.

America's newest voters are its swing voters

United States · sheet 3 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. The Economist's cover argues that Hispanic and Asian Americans are now the country's most dynamic swing voters. Its title: "The Americans changing politics for the better".
  2. America votes on Tuesday in the midterms. Silver Bulletin's forecast gave Democrats a 95.1% chance of winning the House, and 77.7% for the Senate.
  3. Pew counts 39m Hispanic eligible voters in 2026, up 5.2m since 2022. That is about 16% of the electorate.
  4. Asian Americans are the fastest-growing group: 13.7m eligible voters, up about 20%, or about 5% of the electorate.
  5. In 2024 both groups moved sharply toward Trump. In 2025 Hispanic voters in New Jersey and Virginia swung back toward Democrats by about 25 points, Salon and CBS reported.
  6. Polls this autumn suggest the swing back is holding. An NBC News/Telemundo poll in September gave Democrats a 24-point lead among Latino registered voters.
Go deeper: the numbers behind the swing

2020 to 2024. Pew's study of validated voters found that Trump's share of Hispanic voters rose from 36% to 48%; Harris won the group 51–48. Among Asian voters Trump rose from 30% to 40%; Harris won 57–40. Pew says the shift came largely from who turned out, not from people switching sides.

2025. In New Jersey Mikie Sherrill won Latino voters 68–31, CNN's exit poll found. In Virginia Abigail Spanberger won about 67%. In New York City an exit poll of Asian American voters had Zohran Mamdani at 49.1% against Andrew Cuomo's 33.8%.

2026. A survey by AAPI Data and APIAVote, taken in June and July, gave Democrats 63% to 24% among Asian American registered voters for the House. 90% said they plan to vote, up from 84% in 2022.

A caution. The Hoover Institution argues the 2025 results show continuity: Latino voters often favour Democrats in off-year races. On that view, 2024 was the exception, and the question is which electorate turns out.

California. California's governor's race is Democrat Xavier Becerra against Republican Steve Hilton, to replace Gavin Newsom. Voters also decide 14 statewide measures, including the first statewide tax on billionaires, AP reported.

BHow it worksmove one slider

A group moves an election by two things: how big it is, and how far its vote moves. The effect on the national margin is roughly the group's share of voters times the change in its margin. A small group that swings a lot can matter as much as a big group that barely moves.

CThe argument

The Economist's view. Its cover says these voters are changing politics for the better. Because they are not loyal to either party, both parties must compete for them. A related Economist piece says Democrats are rediscovering what an appealing politics of progress looks like.

The strongest counter-view. Swing voters are also unpredictable voters. Their swings follow prices and the economy more than ideas, so they reward whoever is out of power. That could make policy swing with them, rather than improve it.

DWhat to watch
  • Tuesday's results in heavily Hispanic districts in Texas, Florida and California. A big swing back would support the cover; a small one would mean 2024's shift lasted.
  • Turnout among Hispanic and Asian voters. Pew found 2024's shift came largely from who voted, so turnout may matter more than persuasion.
  • California's billionaire tax. If it passes, it is the first statewide tax of its kind, and a test of how far these voters want the state to go.

Brazil: Lula's comeback, and the markets' verdict

The Americas · sheet 4 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Sunday Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won Brazil's presidential run-off with about 51.8% of valid votes. Flávio Bolsonaro won about 48.2%.
  2. Flávio had led the first round on 4 October by more than 2.2m votes. No one who lost the first round had won the presidency since 1989.
  3. Markets had expected Flávio to win. On Monday São Paulo's main index, the Ibovespa, closed down about 13.9%. Trading was halted at one point.
  4. The real lost more than 8% against the dollar on Monday, MercoPress reported.
  5. Also on Monday Flávio said he would ask the electoral court to annul the result. His party said it accepts the certified count.
  6. The Economist's leader says markets are dismayed by Lula's win, "and for good reason".
Go deeper: why a vote moved markets so far

The surprise. Prices are bets on the future. Investors had bet heavily on a Flávio win, and CNBC reported that prediction markets had him ahead. When the less expected result came in, prices had to move all at once. The size of the fall measures the surprise as much as the policy.

The policy fear. Investors worry that a fourth Lula term means more public spending and a weaker hold on the budget. A weaker currency makes imports dearer, which pushes up inflation. Brazil's central bank may then have to keep interest rates high.

The dispute. Flávio's party, the PL, said it accepts the tallies but wants answers about social spending before the vote. NBC, citing sources, reported that Flávio then left for the United States. Lula, 81, began his first term in 2003.

The Economist's ask. "Lula has little time to persuade investors that he will change course in his next stint as president," The Economist wrote on X. It says he must first attend to "an urgent task" to fix the economy.

CThe argument

The strongest counter-view. Part of Monday's fall was the unwinding of one-sided bets, not a verdict on Lula. Prices had risen on the expectation of a Flávio win, so some of the fall gave back those gains. Lula has governed Brazil twice before without a debt crisis.

DWhat to watch

  • The electoral court's answer to Flávio's request. A quick rejection ends the dispute; a long one adds political risk to the economic risk.
  • Whether the central bank raises rates to defend the real. That would slow the economy Lula wants to grow.

Britain's Budget: small moves, and a new tax break for founders

Britain · sheet 5 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Wednesday John Healey presented his first Budget as Chancellor, for Prime Minister Andy Burnham.
  2. The OBR put his headroom at £15bn, down from £23.6bn in March. Healey blamed the cost of the war with Iran.
  3. The OBR cut its forecast for growth in 2027 to 1.0%, from 1.6%, citing trade disruption from the war.
  4. The main measures: a higher bank surcharge, 2p more income tax on savings and property income, and a one-off £100 energy payment for about 20m households in England.
  5. For startups, a new Founders' Relief taxes up to £10m of a founder's lifetime gains at 10%. The older relief for business owners is halved.
  6. On Friday Burnham told ITV that "the room for manoeuvre wasn't what I would have hoped". The Economist's leader says his government "talks of change, then does nothing".
Go deeper: the forecasts, the fine print and the reaction

The numbers. Borrowing is forecast at 4.2% of GDP this year and 3.4% next. Debt interest is £14bn higher this year than forecast in March. The OBR says the Budget's own measures do little for growth. Healey left headline rates of income tax, National Insurance and VAT unchanged.

The fine print for founders. The £10m cap is reduced by any Business Asset Disposal Relief or Investors' Relief claimed from April 2027. The firm must be a high-growth, innovative UK company. A technical consultation closes on 30 November. The law firm Mishcon de Reya says the conditions are so tight that the relief will rarely apply, and it will not help most holders of share options.

What was put off. Welfare reform and the path for defence spending were left to the 2027 spending review. Healey said he would fill a £4.7bn hole in the defence investment plan by moving money within capital budgets. The Institute for Government said the scorecard had almost 100 policies, most of them very small.

The reaction. The Institute for Government called the Budget "big in size but small in ambition". The IFS said it was "broadly the Budget that many were expecting". Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, said: "The Prime Minister came in promising that he would 'reverse 40 years of neoliberalism'. Instead, we got 4p off gherkins."

Markets. Gilt yields held steady, with the 10-year near 5.3%. The pound ended Friday at about $1.339, roughly 0.1% higher on the week.

BHow it worksslide, then switch

A founder who sells pays capital gains tax on the gain. Until now the first £1m could be taxed at 18% under BADR, and the rest at 24%. From April 2027 a qualifying founder pays 10% on up to £10m. A founder who does not qualify keeps BADR, but only on the first £500,000. Slide the gain and switch the rules.

CThe argument

Britain's unimpressive budget reveals a lack of ambition.The Economist, leader, 29 October

The Economist's view. Burnham promised change, but his first Budget is a list of small measures. The hard choices on welfare, defence and taxes were pushed into 2027.

The strongest counter-view. With £15bn of headroom, a war pushing up prices and gilt yields above 5%, a dull Budget was the safe one. A fund manager at Guinness called it "the boring Budget bond markets wanted". Gilts held steady, which a bold Budget might not have managed.

DWhat to watch
  • The Founders' Relief consultation, closing 30 November. Broad rules would make it a real draw for founders; narrow ones would leave most founders worse off once BADR is halved.
  • The Bank of England on Thursday. Markets expect a rate rise from 3.75%; a rise would push gilt yields and the cost of debt higher, eating into the £15bn buffer.
  • The 2027 spending review. That is where the deferred choices on welfare and defence must be made.

Oil keeps central banks on edge: the Fed splits, Canada hikes

Finance and economics · sheet 6 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Wednesday the Federal Reserve held its rate at 3.75–4.00%. The vote was 10–2.
  2. Governor Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack of the Cleveland Fed dissented. Both wanted a rate rise of 25 basis points. Fortune said a governor and a regional president last dissented together in 1992.
  3. The same day the Bank of Canada raised its rate to 2.50%, its first rise since July 2023.
  4. On Thursday the ECB held its deposit rate at 2.50%. Christine Lagarde said its council had debated a rise.
  5. Also on Thursday America's preferred inflation measure, the PCE price index, rose to 3.6% for September. GDP grew at an annualised 3.4% in the third quarter.
  6. On Friday the Bank of Japan held at 1.25%. Brent crude traded near $118 a barrel, against $99.47 a month earlier.
Go deeper: the oil shock and the 'second round'

The source. Oil is high because of the war with Iran. On Friday Iran's Revolutionary Guards said tankers had hit mines near the Strait of Hormuz; America's Central Command called the claims false. Brent rose about 13–14% in October, OilPrice reported.

The worry. A rise in oil prices lifts inflation once. Central banks usually look through that. The danger is the second round: workers demand higher pay and firms raise other prices. US core PCE rose to 3.1% from 3.0%, a sign that the spread may have started.

Canada's reasoning. Governor Tiff Macklem said a rise was needed to reduce the risk that high energy prices "spill over and become generalized inflation", CBC and BNN reported. The bank now expects inflation of 3.1% in 2027; in July it expected 2.0%.

The Fed's chair. Kevin Warsh gave no guidance on the next move and said politics played "no role" ahead of the midterms. He also said the Fed will hold fewer rate-setting meetings: from next autumn one of the eight a year becomes a strategy meeting. Futures point to a rise at the next meeting, on 9 December.

Europe and Japan. National flash figures for October showed inflation of 5.4% in Italy, 4.9% in Spain, 3.4% in France and 2.8% in Germany, where a fuel-tax cut held prices down. The Bank of Japan said for the first time since 2013 that underlying inflation is "generally consistent" with its 2% target. One of its nine members voted for a rise.

CHow it works

Why hold, why hike. A central bank cannot pump more oil. Raising rates does nothing to the price of a barrel. It works by cooling demand, so firms find it harder to pass on costs and workers find it harder to win big rises.

The trade-off. If the bank waits and the shock fades, it avoids a needless slowdown. If it waits and prices spread, it must raise rates further later. Canada and the two Fed dissenters judged the second risk bigger. The Fed's majority and the ECB chose to wait one more meeting.

The link to sheet 1. Higher rates also raise what AI builders pay to borrow. Warsh said this week that some credit spreads on AI-related debt have widened.

DWhat to watch
  • The Bank of England on Thursday 5 November. Markets almost fully expect a rise from 3.75%; holding would surprise them and weaken the pound.
  • Euro-area inflation for October on Wednesday. A figure above September's 3.8% would make a December rise by the ECB very likely.
  • Talks on reopening Hormuz. Iran's foreign minister spoke of a two-day plan on Saturday. A real reopening would cut oil prices and take pressure off every central bank on this sheet.

AI's boldest claims yet, and who checks them

Science and AI · sheet 7 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Monday OpenAI announced three results it says its models produced, in computer science, mathematics and physics.
  2. The first is an algorithm that counts the solutions of a class of logic puzzles in subexponential time. If correct, it refutes the Exponential Time Hypothesis.
  3. The second is a claimed proof of Christopher Sogge's 1991 local smoothing conjecture in all dimensions. That would settle the Kakeya and restriction conjectures too.
  4. The third claims a rigorous construction of certain interacting quantum field theories in four dimensions, including the theory of the strong force.
  5. OpenAI said the logic result was found on 16 October and checked in Lean on 17 October.
  6. None of the three has been peer-reviewed. Quanta noted that OpenAI released them in very different ways.
Go deeper: how each claim is being checked

The logic result. Russell Impagliazzo, Ramamohan Paturi and Ryan Williams are organising an open review. Impagliazzo and Paturi proposed ETH in the first place. Williams said the reviewers signed no non-disclosure agreements and are unpaid. OpenAI says practical algorithms would need significant further research.

The maths result. Human progress had been slow but real. Local smoothing was proved in two dimensions in 2019, and Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl proved the Kakeya conjecture in three dimensions in 2025. The new claim covers all dimensions and is reported to come with a public Lean proof.

The physics result. This is the least open. An external group led by the Nobel physicist David Gross is evaluating it, but the materials are being withheld and there is no public Lean proof. Proving that such theories exist is a step towards one of the Clay Millennium Prize problems, but the claim does not include the prize's "mass gap".

Why checking is hard. Lean checks the proof, not whether the formal statement matches the real conjecture. A separate preprint argued that OpenAI's earlier Navier–Stokes formalisation did not match its prose. In October Quanta reported that a first, Lean-checked version of an AI proof of a quasi-Riemann hypothesis was set aside.

Context. On 6 October OpenAI released 722 manuscripts covering 372 families of results, the story this brief led on in its 11 October edition. This week's claims are fewer and far bigger.

Why does "subexponential" matter if it is still slow? Because it changes the shape of the curve. An exponential cost multiplies with each extra variable; a subexponential one eventually grows more slowly than any such multiplier. But "eventually" can be very far away. Slide the problem size.

CThe argument

The case for excitement. If even one claim holds, AI has solved a problem that top humans failed to crack for decades. The logic result is open to checking by the very people who framed the problem.

The case for caution. Big claims, released by the company that gains from them, before peer review. The physics materials are withheld. Lean guards against bad steps but not against proving the wrong statement, and one earlier AI proof has already been set aside.

Science notebook: silk, restless space, and head injuries

Science · sheet 9 of 10
AHow spiders make the toughest fibre

This week. Quanta reported on Wednesday how spiders turn a liquid "dope" into silk. Researchers used NMR, a method that reads the motion of atoms, on the liquid inside the spider's silk glands.

What they found. Adding phosphate makes the silk proteins gather into dense droplets, called condensates. Weak contacts between two kinds of amino acid, arginine and tyrosine, hold the droplets together in the floppy parts of the proteins.

Go deeper: why it matters

The old picture. Silk's strength has long been put down to stiff, stacked "beta-sheets" in the finished fibre. The new work suggests the strength also depends on how the liquid is organised before it is spun.

The use. Labs have struggled to make artificial silk as tough as a spider's. If the recipe depends on the liquid stage, copying that stage may matter more than copying the final protein.

B"Empty" space is not calm

This week. Quanta reported on Friday on a new 3D map of the gas between the stars, the interstellar medium, across thousands of light-years.

What it shows. Much of the gas is unstable and constantly churned by heating and cooling. That challenges the textbook picture of a few calm, balanced states of gas.

Go deeper: how the map was made

The method. The team combined a 3D map of dust, built from the Gaia telescope's 3D map of stars, with a map of the ultraviolet light that heats the gas.

Why it matters. Stars form from cold, dense gas. If heating and cooling keep stirring it, fewer clouds settle long enough to collapse, which would help explain why the galaxy forms stars as slowly as it does.

How empty is empty. Very. The New Horizons probe measured about 130 hydrogen atoms per litre of space near the Sun, Quanta reported. Air holds about 10²² molecules per litre.

CThree in four NFL players died with CTE

This week. A study published on Wednesday estimated that 74.5% of former NFL players who died between 2008 and 2023 had CTE. It covered 2,362 deaths.

Why the number is new. Earlier figures came only from donated brains, and families donate when they suspect a problem. Among donors the rate was 93.5%. The new study adjusts for that selection.

Go deeper: the evidence and its limits

Dementia. The study also estimated that 27.2% had dementia in life, which the authors call likely an underestimate.

The limits. The estimate applies to players who have died, not to living ones. An adjustment for selection rests on assumptions about who donates; a different model would give a different number.

The lesson. It is a clean example of selection bias: a sample chosen because of a symptom overstates how common the symptom is. The correction lowered the estimate from 93.5% to 74.5%, and it is still very high.

The wonder: a 240-year-old voting paradox has a shape

Wonder · sheet 10 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. In 1785 the French mathematician Nicolas de Condorcet found that a majority can vote itself into a loop.
  2. Three voters rank three options. One prefers A to B to C, one B to C to A, and one C to A to B. Then a majority prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. No option beats all the others.
  3. On Monday Quanta reported that three researchers have found the shape of this paradox: a Klein bottle, a surface with no inside or outside.
  4. They are Ori Livson, a doctoral student at the University of Sydney, his adviser Mikhail Prokopenko, and Siddharth Pritam of the Chennai Mathematical Institute.
  5. Their key result: a model of preferences is non-orientable exactly when it models contradictory cycles. In one variant the surface is the real projective plane instead.
  6. The paper is published in the journal Mathematics.
Go deeper: how the bottle is built

The tube. The team builds a tube whose two rims each stand for a full cycle of preferences. Going round a cycle implies that every option ranks above every other, so the two rims mean the same thing. "Once you realize two things are the same, you can glue them together," Livson told Quanta.

The twist. "Gluing with the twist is the only valid option," he said. Gluing a tube's ends with a twist that reverses direction is exactly how a Klein bottle is made. The twist is the contradiction: going round the loop brings you back with left and right swapped.

The family tree. In 1951 Kenneth Arrow proved that no voting rule can turn individual rankings into a group ranking while meeting a few fair-sounding conditions. In 1980 Graciela Chichilnisky began to treat social choice as topology, and mused that Condorcet's paradox itself is a topological problem. Livson and Prokopenko have a companion paper that recasts Arrow's theorem in these terms.

The man. Condorcet was a champion of reason and democracy during the French Revolution. He was arrested during the Terror and died in prison in 1794.

CWhy it matters

For elections. Cycles are not just a puzzle. When they occur, the order in which options are voted on decides the winner. A chair who controls the agenda can then pick the outcome.

For machines. The same problem appears when AI systems combine many rankings, such as human preferences in training. Any method must break cycles somehow, and the choice is not neutral.

For maths. Turning a paradox into a shape lets topologists' tools, such as orientability, test which voting set-ups can be consistent at all.
