Liberal Europe's agony: France after the riots

Europe · sheet 1 of 8
AWhat happened
  1. On Saturday 17 October, the day before this week began, protests across France turned into riots. The interior ministry counted 2,397 arrests and 997 police and gendarmes injured.
  2. Looters ransacked the BHV Marais department store in Paris. Limoges city hall was set on fire. One protester died of cardiac arrest.
  3. On Sunday Emmanuel Macron said on television that "nothing justifies" the "unbridled" violence. He ordered a review of police readiness.
  4. On Monday partial curfews began in parts of Paris and Lyon. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said more would follow where necessary.
  5. On Thursday Macron said "illegitimate" protests would be banned in the coming days.
  6. The Economist's cover calls this "the agony of liberal Europe": centrist rulers "have fallen dangerously short—and are now paying the price".
Go deeper: why the protests and the budget are the same story

What protesters want. The marches were about the cost of living, petrol prices, budget cuts and run-down schools, France 24 reported. They began in schools: more than 400 closed on a single Friday, Al Jazeera reported. More than 500,000 people marched on 17 October.

What lenders want. The government plans a €54bn budget squeeze, including a pay freeze for public-sector workers. France's debt is about €3.6tn, about 119% of GDP. On Thursday CNBC reported that insuring French debt against default now costs more than insuring Italy's.

Who gains. Marine Le Pen of the National Rally called the violence "a threat to democracy" and attacked a "Macronist administration that is out of steam, exhausted, and disoriented". The Economist argues the violence will benefit the extremes. The government blames far-left groups for most of the looting.

The clock. Macron is in his last months in office. The presidential election is due in about six months. A government that must cut spending now faces voters who want more of it, just before they choose his successor.

Beyond France. The Economist also reports unruly protests in Albania and Italy, which this brief could not check in wire reports. In Germany, Chancellor Friedrich Merz told the youth wing of his own party on 17 October that pension reform is "overdue" ("überfällig"). The Economist says its young members are angry with him.

BHow it worksmove the sliders

Why can't France just borrow to pay protesters? Because debt has its own momentum. Each year the pile grows by the interest on it, and shrinks as a share of the economy when the economy grows. If the interest rate is above growth, debt rises even with a balanced budget before interest. Move the sliders.

CThe argument

Centrist rulers have fallen dangerously short—and are now paying the price.The Economist, leader, 22 October

The Economist's view. Mainstream governments promised more than they could pay for. Now voters are angry and the money has run out, and the extremes promise both order and more spending.

The strongest counter-view. The riots are a cost-of-living revolt more than a verdict on centrism. Petrol prices are high because of the war in the Gulf, which no French government caused. On this view, cheaper oil would calm the streets faster than any reform.

Saudi Arabia and the UAE: a rivalry that feeds other people's wars

Middle East and Africa · sheet 2 of 8
AWhat happened
  1. The Economist says Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates "are sowing chaos". Its briefing says their rivalry is prolonging wars in Sudan, Yemen and elsewhere.
  2. In Sudan, the army fights the RSF. Saudi Arabia backs the army.
  3. The UN human-rights office counted at least 306 civilians killed by drones in Sudan in the first three weeks of October.
  4. The worst single strike, on Sunday, hit a truck in West Kordofan and killed 139 people. No side was named.
  5. In Yemen, the Houthis fired two missiles at Aden airport on Saturday, their fourth attack on it this month.
  6. The UAE has reportedly agreed to fund anti-Houthi forces in southern Yemen again, with Saudi air cover. Neither government has confirmed it.
Go deeper: how two allies became rivals, and why that matters for Sudan

The split. The two were partners in Yemen's war against the Houthis. In December 2025 the UAE-backed Southern Transitional Council seized the eastern provinces of Hadramawt and Mahra. On 30 December Saudi Arabia struck what it said was an Emirati arms shipment at the port of Mukalla. In January the council was dissolved and the UAE pulled out.

Sudan. A UN fact-finding mission said on 3 September that UAE-linked people and companies played a "significant role" in supplying the RSF. It did not blame the Emirati state, and the UAE denies backing the RSF. On 7 October army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan rejected talks and vowed to retake "every inch" of RSF territory.

Gold. Gold pays for much of the war. Official exports to the UAE fell to 8.2 tonnes in 2025 from 22.2 tonnes in 2024. But Sudan mines about 70 tonnes a year, and the group Sudan Transparency says much of it is now sent through Egypt.

The human cost. About 13 million Sudanese have fled their homes, counting refugees abroad. ReliefWeb calls it the world's largest displacement crisis. By mid-year the UN's aid appeal had about 40% of the money it asked for.

Saudi Arabia's bad year. On Monday Saudi Arabia postponed its big FII investment conference to 2027. The Economist reports the IMF now expects the Saudi economy to shrink by 3.3% this year. On Thursday Muhammad bin Salman's state oil firm, Aramco, told staff to leave its Dhahran headquarters after Houthi strikes.

BHow it worksmove the sliders

Wars end in a deal when one side expects to lose by fighting on. Outside backers change that sum: they replace a client's losses. When rival patrons back both sides, each side's losses are replaced, and neither runs short. Try it.

CThe argument

Gulf powers should stop fuelling carnage in their backyard.The Economist, leader, 22 October

The Economist's view. Two rich allies of the West are now rivals. Their competition keeps wars going in Sudan and Yemen, and both should stop.

The other side. Each says it is containing a threat. Saudi Arabia backs governments it calls legitimate. The UAE says it opposes Islamist movements and denies arming the RSF. The reported deal in Yemen suggests a shared enemy, the Houthis, can still bring them together.

Europe's satellite problem: the Starlink cut-off as a test

Space · sheet 3 of 8
AWhat happened
  1. On 13–14 October SpaceX limited Starshield terminals in Ukraine that move faster than about 160 km/h. That hit drones and aircraft.
  2. Who ordered it is disputed. On Friday 16 October Reuters said the order came from the White House. On Tuesday the Wall Street Journal said Elon Musk ordered it himself. Trump denied knowing in advance.
  3. On Tuesday Volodymyr Zelensky said the limits could be lifted "within two days". Ukraine's defence ministry later said Starshield worked again above 400 km/h.
  4. On Thursday nine US senators from both parties asked the Pentagon's inspector general to investigate.
  5. The Economist calls the cut-offs "a test", and says "worse may follow". Its leader says Europe must escape its reliance on American satellites.
  6. On Friday four big European phone companies, among them Deutsche Telekom and Orange, agreed to explore a joint bid for spectrum to link satellites straight to phones.
Go deeper: what Europe has, and how far behind it is

The gap in orbit. Starlink passed 10,000 satellites in March. Europe's Eutelsat OneWeb has about 650, roughly one sixteenth as many. On Wednesday SpaceX reported $5.2bn of Starlink revenue in the third quarter and 13.6 million consumer subscribers.

Europe's plan. IRIS² will have 348 satellites. Its cost has risen from €10.5bn at signing in December 2024 to €15.6bn, about 47% more, before any satellite has flown. Services are due in 2030.

Rockets. Europe flew four Ariane 6 rockets and two Vega C rockets in 2026 to the end of August. Falcon 9 flew 96 times by mid-August. Ariane 6 throws its whole rocket away; Falcon 9 lands and reuses its first stage. European governments pay €290m–340m a year to support Ariane 6.

Old habits. Under ESA's geo-return rule, each country's money comes back to its own firms. Eutelsat's chief told the French parliament in March that "made in Europe" rules strain IRIS²'s cost and schedule.

Some progress. Isar Aerospace's Spectrum rocket reached orbit on 5 September, the first orbital launch from continental Europe. ESA's members agreed a record budget of about €22bn in November 2025.

BHow it worksmove the sliders

Why can't Europe just build its own Starlink? Launch is the bottleneck. A rocket's fixed costs (factories, launch pads, staff) are the same whether it flies 6 times a year or 150, and an expendable rocket throws away its hardware every flight. SpaceX launches its own Starlink satellites, so it has the cadence that makes reuse pay.

CThe argument

Europe must escape its reliance on American satellites. To do so it will have to drop old habits.The Economist, leader, 22 October

The Economist's view. Ukraine shows that a link run by one foreign firm can be switched off. Europe should build its own capacity, and stop splitting space work between countries for political reasons.

The strongest counter-view. Europe does not need a Starlink copy. It needs a secure network for governments, which IRIS² is meant to be, and it can buy commercial capacity from several firms. Duplicating a 10,000-satellite network would cost far more than the risk it removes.

Indonesia: Prabowo's charm fades, and so does investors' trust

Asia · sheet 4 of 8
AWhat happened
  1. A poll by SMRC, published by Monday, put approval of President Prabowo Subianto at 44.5%. It is the first time it has fallen below half.
  2. On Tuesday he marked two years in office. The Economist says his "erratic policies are holding back his country".
  3. On Friday 16 October MSCI said it may take Indonesia out of its emerging-markets index. It cited weak disclosure of who owns shares.
  4. Jakarta's index fell as much as 11% that day, its worst day since January 1998. On Monday it fell 3.64% more, to its lowest since October 2020.
  5. On Wednesday Bank Indonesia held its main rate at 5.75%. It raised the rate it pays banks on deposits and cut the cash banks must hold in reserve.
  6. The rupiah traded near 18,000 to the dollar, about 7.9% weaker than at the start of the year.
Go deeper: populism, the central bank and the index

The polls. SMRC's approval figure was 51.1% in July, 66.4% in March and 81.2% in November 2025. A different poll, by PPI, gave 65.2% on Sunday; the two firms use different methods. In SMRC's poll only 9.8% would back Prabowo for the 2029 election.

The central bank. In June parliament changed the law on the financial sector. It added economic growth to the central bank's goals and let lawmakers evaluate its performance. Critics saw a threat to the bank's independence. The bank has raised its rate by a full point this year, most recently in June.

The budget. Indonesia has a legal limit on its budget deficit of 3% of GDP. A parliamentary panel put this year's deficit at 2.85%, close to the limit. The free school-meals scheme had its 2026 budget cut to Rp229trn from Rp335trn, Reuters reported; it has been linked to more than 50,000 cases of food poisoning.

The index. MSCI will take comments until 30 April 2027 and decide by 30 June 2027. Estimates of the money that would leave differ: about $3bn from index-copying funds alone (Mirae, based on a 0.4% index weight), up to $13bn in all (Goldman Sachs), or $4bn plus about $15bn from active funds (UBS).

A counterpoint. Factories and firms keep coming. Foreign direct investment rose about 27.5% in the third quarter from a year earlier, the Jakarta Post reported. The money leaving fastest is portfolio money, which can sell in a day.

AI or debt: two market fears that may cancel out

Markets · sheet 5 of 8
AWhat happened
  1. The Economist's leader says either AI or public debt could crash markets, "but probably not both". Its reason: "If AI stocks collapse, bond yields will fall."
  2. On Wednesday evening Meta said it will spend "a lot more" on AI in 2027. On Thursday its shares fell about 7% and chip shares fell 4–6%.
  3. Investors moved money into US government bonds. The 10-year Treasury yield fell below 5% on Thursday, from 5.15% on Monday.
  4. On Friday oil jumped, and the yield rose back to about 5.00%. Brent crude closed at $116.72 a barrel.
  5. On Wednesday a bond of CoreWeave, a firm that rents out AI computing, hit a record yield of 15.76%. Lenders are charging AI borrowers much more.
  6. The Federal Reserve meets on Wednesday 28 October.
Go deeper: why the two fears pull in opposite directions

The hedge. A crash in AI shares would scare investors into safe government bonds. Their buying pushes yields down, so governments borrow more cheaply. The fear that debt will become unpayable would then ease. So the two crashes are unlikely to come together. This is The Economist's argument.

This week's test. Thursday showed the hedge at work: shares fell and yields fell. One Reuters report put the 10-year's fall at 19 basis points, the most in a day since March 2023, though other reports gave less. Friday showed the exception: oil pushed inflation fears up, and yields rose back.

The exception. If inflation stays high, the Fed cannot cut rates to support markets. Then yields can stay high even as shares fall. On Friday futures prices gave about 7% odds of another Fed rise, down from 27.7% a week earlier.

Where the risk sits. CoreWeave's shares are about 70% below their peak. CNBC put Nvidia's exposure to firms like it at about $171.5bn, mostly guarantees and commitments rather than cash. On Thursday CNBC reported that insuring French government debt now costs more than insuring Italy's (sheet 1).

BHow it workstoggle, then slide

Either AI or public debt could crash markets. But probably not both.The Economist, leader, 21 October

The Economist's view. Investors worry about two bubbles at once. But an AI crash would push money into government bonds, which helps indebted governments.

The strongest counter-view. The hedge needs low inflation. With Brent near $117 and the Fed raising rates this year, a crash could come with high yields. A crash would also cut tax revenue, which makes debts harder to pay even if borrowing is cheaper.

  • The Fed on Wednesday. A hold would leave room for yields to fall if shares slide; another rise would point to the "both" case.
  • Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon report next week. Bigger AI spending plans could repeat Thursday's pattern.
  • Britain's Budget on Wednesday, a test of whether lenders still fund big deficits without charging more.

Israel votes on Tuesday: a lead is not a government

Middle East · sheet 6 of 8
AWhat happened
  1. Israel holds a general election on Tuesday 27 October. The Knesset has 120 seats, so a government needs 61.
  2. The final TV polls came out on Friday. All three main ones put Gadi Eisenkot's party, Yashar, first, with 22–23 seats.
  3. They gave his camp 52–56 seats and Benjamin Netanyahu's camp about 50. Neither reaches 61.
  4. Arab-majority parties get 14–16 seats. Last month the Supreme Court overturned their disqualification.
  5. A party needs 3.25% of the vote to win any seats. That threshold decides the fate of small parties such as the Reservists.
  6. The Economist calls Israel "deeply divided" and the outcome "deeply uncertain".
Go deeper: how polls become (or fail to become) a government

The polls. Channel 12 gave Eisenkot's camp 56 seats and Netanyahu's 50. Channel 13 gave 54 to 50, and Kan, the public broadcaster, 52 to 50. Channel 14, which leans right, was the outlier: it gave Likud 29 seats and Netanyahu's camp 61.

The threshold. The Reservists win four seats in Kan's poll and none in Channel 12's. When a party falls below the line, its votes are wasted and the other parties share the seats. A few thousand votes can move a whole bloc.

The issues. The main fights are over drafting Haredi men into the army and over security after the long war with Iran and its allies.

A past test. In 2021 a coalition led by Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid included an Arab party, Ra'am, for the first time. It fell after about a year. Bennett and Lapid now run together on one list.

Science notebook: a trip to Phobos, and a better second

Science · sheet 7 of 8
AJapan heads for a moon of Mars

This week. Japan's MMX probe lifted off on an H3 rocket from Tanegashima at 04:41 local time on Tuesday 20 October. It aims to be the first spacecraft to orbit Phobos and bring back a sample of it.

Why it matters. No one knows where Mars's moons came from. They may be captured asteroids, or debris from a giant impact on Mars. A sample of the soil will show which.

Go deeper: the plan, step by step

The journey. MMX should reach Mars in 2027. It will study Phobos from orbit, land, and collect at least 10 grams of material. It will also fly past the smaller moon, Deimos.

The return. The sample should reach Earth in 2031. France's space agency CNES and Germany's DLR built a small rover for it; ESA helps with ground stations.

Why Phobos. Phobos may carry dust blasted off Mars by impacts. A sample could therefore hold bits of Mars too, without the cost of landing on the planet and taking off again.

Read more: ESA: MMX lifts off ↗ · Wikipedia: Martian Moons eXploration ↗ · The Economist: Japan's mission to Mars's moons ↗

BA new definition of the second

The news. The Economist reports that "the second may soon be redefined", with several types of atomic clock competing for the job.

Why. Since 1967 the second has been defined by an atom of caesium. Newer optical clocks tick far faster, so they divide time much more finely.

Go deeper: what has to happen first

The conditions. The body that keeps the units, BIPM, says optical clocks must be about 100 times better than caesium ones, agree with the old definition, and feed regularly into world time.

The timetable. BIPM's earliest dates: a proposal from 2026, and ratification of a new definition in 2030 at the earliest.

The open question. Scientists have not agreed which atom to use. One idea is to define the second from a weighted mix of several clocks rather than one atom.

How malaria breaks into a blood cell in under a minute

Wonder · sheet 8 of 8
AWhat happened
  1. A team led by Chi-Min Ho at Columbia University filmed, at the scale of molecules, how the malaria parasite gets into a red blood cell, Quanta reported on Friday.
  2. They used cryo-electron microscopy to catch the break-in at each stage.
  3. The whole entry takes under a minute.
  4. In test tubes, a protein designed with AI blocked the entry. It has not been tested in animals.
Go deeper: the break-in, step by step

The doorway. The parasite pushes a ring of protein anchors, called the moving junction, into the cell's skin. Each anchor has four protein units: three form a dense wedge under the membrane, and the fourth ties it to the parasite.

The push. The parasite locks onto the anchors and pushes forward. Electric forces push the membrane's fat molecules aside and flip its curve, so it bows inward around the parasite.

The seal. The parasite slips through a temporary portal, and the membrane closes behind it. Inside, it multiplies for about 48 hours before the cell bursts.

Why it matters. "If you block invasion, then you stop the parasite in its tracks," Ho said. Malaria kills about 600,000 people a year. Because the entry is so fast, a drug would have to act very quickly or stay in the blood. The study was partly funded by the Simons Foundation, which also funds Quanta.

Read more: Quanta: malaria parasite caught in the act ↗

BWhy it matters

Drugs usually attack the parasite once it is inside a cell. A drug that blocks the doorway would stop it before it can multiply. Knowing the exact shape of the anchors tells designers where to aim.
