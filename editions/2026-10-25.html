The Gulf: a record month of attacks, and oil at $117

The world · sheet 1 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Overnight on Wednesday, Iran's state TV said seven oil tankers were hit or struck mines in and near the Strait of Hormuz. No one has confirmed the claim.
  2. October already has the most ship attacks of the war: 28 by the Washington Institute's count, 27 by the UN shipping agency's.
  3. On Thursday Saudi Aramco told staff to leave its headquarters in Dhahran after strikes by the Houthis, Reuters reported.
  4. An attack on Dammam's main airport killed two people on Thursday. On Wednesday drone debris killed two workers at the Shaybah oilfield.
  5. On Friday Brent crude settled at $116.72 a barrel, its highest since April. It rose about 6% in the week.
  6. Donald Trump is weighing new strikes on Iran. On 8 October he said there would be none before the 3 November midterms.
Go deeper: a war at sea that now reaches Saudi Arabia's oil heartland

The claim. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said they hit four tankers while the ships were moving oil between each other in the northern Gulf. They said three more struck mines west of the strait. NBC, Al Jazeera and PBS all reported the claim and said it was unverified. Trump answered on social media: "Iran is doing everything they can to drive up the price of Oil prior to the U.S. Midterm Election."

The record. Noam Raydan of the Washington Institute told USNI News: "October has seen the highest number of ship attacks on record." The UN's International Maritime Organization counts 27 attacks so far this month. It says 11 VLCCs were damaged in October alone.

The Saudi front. Reuters cited six sources on the Aramco evacuation; reports differ on whether it was full or partial. Most staff had worked from home since an earlier strike damaged part of one tower. The Saudi energy ministry said the Shaybah drone was aimed at a processing plant and did not affect output. America's State Department ordered non-emergency staff and their families to leave Saudi Arabia.

The talks. America and Iran talk indirectly, through Pakistan and Qatar. On Tuesday Pakistan's army chief, Asim Munir, visited Tehran. Iran said there could be no deal without sanctions relief. On Wednesday its foreign minister said talks go on but are stuck on Iran's terms for reopening Hormuz. On Saturday Iran blamed America for the failure and urged a new try.

The threats. On Saturday Iran's armed forces chief, Ali Abdollahi, said American warships would not be allowed in the Gulf "under any circumstances and at any time", Al Jazeera reported. The Revolutionary Guards also threatened undersea internet cables if Iran's civilian sites are hit again. America's ambassador to Turkey, Tom Barrack, said America could "absolutely" strike as soon as this weekend.

AI fear pulls money into bonds, then oil pushes back

Markets and money · sheet 2 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Monday America's 10-year Treasury yield stood at 5.15%.
  2. On Wednesday evening Meta said it will spend "a lot more" on AI in 2027. On Thursday its shares fell about 7%, and chip stocks fell 4–6%.
  3. Investors sold AI shares and bought Treasuries. Buying bonds pushes their yields down, and the 10-year fell below 5% on Thursday.
  4. On Friday oil jumped (sheet 1). Dearer oil means higher inflation, so the 10-year rose back to about 5.00%.
  5. That is 9 basis points lower than a week earlier. The S&P 500 fell about 0.5% in the week, its second weekly fall in a row.
  6. On Wednesday Britain's inflation rose to 3.5% in September, a week before the Budget.
Go deeper: two forces pulling bond yields in opposite directions

The tug of war. Two forces now move US yields. Fear about AI debts sends money into Treasuries, which pushes yields down. Oil and inflation push them up. On Thursday the first force won; on Friday the second. The 10-year had hit 5.36% on 7 October, its highest since 2002, CNBC reported.

Thursday. Meta's revenue rose 26%, but it raised its spending forecast again. Applied Materials and KLA, which make chip-making tools, fell about 6%. The Nasdaq fell about 2%. One Reuters report put the 10-year's fall at 19 basis points, the biggest in a day since March 2023. Other reports gave a smaller fall, so treat the size as uncertain.

Friday. The selling spread from tech to older industries. The Dow fell 652.82 points. American Express fell more than 3% after saying it will cut about 5% of its staff to "prepare for AI". Reuters says S&P 500 company profits are on course to grow more than 32% in the third quarter, which is why shares have not fallen further.

The Fed. The Federal Reserve, led by Kevin Warsh, meets on Wednesday. Its rate is 3.75–4.00% after a rise in September. On Friday futures prices gave about 7% odds of another rise, down from 27.7% a week earlier. Goldman Sachs expects a pause now and "a second and final hike in December".

Britain. Core inflation, which leaves out food and energy, rose to 2.8%. Services inflation rose to 3.7%. Petrol and diesel averaged 169.5p a litre, the highest since August 2022. The government borrowed £24.5bn in September and paid £10.9bn in debt interest. Net debt passed £3 trillion for the first time, the ONS said. Most economists expect Bank Rate to rise to 4.00% on 5 November.

Ukraine: Witkoff meets Putin, and a US peace plan leaks

The world · sheet 3 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Sunday Ukraine sent more than 1,000 drones into Russia. An oil terminal in St Petersburg caught fire, and one person died.
  2. On Wednesday Steve Witkoff, Trump's envoy, met Vladimir Putin in St Petersburg. Jared Kushner joined by video.
  3. A Kremlin aide said Russia is open in principle to three-way talks with Ukraine and America next month.
  4. The same day Russia freed Stephen Hubbard, a retired American teacher.
  5. On Thursday The Economist reported a US peace plan, citing Ukrainian officials. It includes a demilitarised zone in Donbas and an end to Ukraine's NATO bid.
  6. By Saturday Ukraine said its Starshield links were back to normal speed, after a week of limits.
Go deeper: drones, a plan on paper, and a satellite switch

The drone war. Russia's defence ministry said it shot down 1,015 Ukrainian drones on Sunday. Kpler data, reported by CNBC, show Russia's diesel exports fell more than 75% to 141,000 barrels a day in September. Ukraine's defence ministry claims more than half of Russia's refining capacity is damaged; that is Ukraine's own figure. On Sunday Trump said Volodymyr Zelenskyy "doesn't want to end this war", CNN reported.

The talks. Yuri Ushakov, a Kremlin aide, said Moscow is willing to resume talks next month. Dmitry Peskov, Putin's spokesman, said the talks will "take a long time". One Ukrainian outlet, Liga.net, gives a window of 10–19 November; no other outlet has confirmed the dates.

The plan. According to The Economist's sources, the plan also lifts sanctions on Russia and lets Russian gas flow to Europe again. Ukraine would get a package for EU entry and investment in return for dropping its NATO bid. Meduza summed up the positions: "Kyiv refuses to surrender territory. Putin insists it was Russia's all along."

Starlink. On Tuesday CNBC reported a Wall Street Journal story that Elon Musk himself ordered the slowdown. Musk wrote on X that Ukraine's military "violated Starlink terms of service and used it for offensive drone strikes". He also said Starshield is controlled by the US government. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the administration did not ask for the limits.

Not fully fixed. Ukraine's defence ministry says Starshield now works on platforms moving faster than 400 km/h. But a source in the Donetsk region told CNN that speeds there were still capped at 160 km/h on Saturday morning.

AI's debt pile: a record CoreWeave yield, and Nvidia's guarantees

The Economist's picks · sheet 4 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. The Economist wrote that "the AI industry's gigantic debts are making investors nervous".
  2. On Wednesday the yield on CoreWeave's 2031 bond hit a record 15.76%. Its shares are about 70% below their peak.
  3. In October shares in two other neoclouds, Nebius and IREN, fell nearly 29% and almost 46%.
  4. On Saturday CNBC put Nvidia's exposure to these firms at about $171.5bn. Most of it is guarantees and commitments, not cash.
  5. Meta created a new class of non-voting shares. Its filing says it may need to raise "hundreds of billions of dollars" over several years.
  6. Matt Levine's Money Stuff column on Tuesday was titled "Someone Has to Take the AI Risk".
Go deeper: who carries the bill if the AI build-out goes wrong

The Economist's numbers. As quoted by others, The Economist counts ten listed neoclouds. Only two make a profit. Their borrowing, about $100bn, is three times their yearly revenue. Its question: "Who will be left to pick up the bill in the event of a crash?" These are The Economist's own figures, not checked independently here.

CoreWeave. Reuters puts CoreWeave's debt at about $50bn. The cost of insuring its debt against default reached about 1,115 basis points, Startup Fortune reported. CNBC says the market for low-rated data-centre debt is effectively shut until after the midterms. Last week its chief executive, Mike Intrator, wrote: "We are confident in our backlog which more than supports all of our debt obligations."

Nvidia. CNBC's $171.5bn is about $7bn of direct investment and $164.5bn of guarantees and commitments. Nvidia sells the chips that neoclouds borrow to buy. If its customers fail, some of the risk comes back to Nvidia.

Meta. Each shareholder will get one non-voting Class C share for each share they hold, in January. Mark Zuckerberg owns about 13% of Meta's value but controls about 61% of the votes, Fortune reported. The new shares let Meta raise money while he keeps control. Levine's Thursday column: "Zuckerberg Wants to Keep His Votes".

The labs. Reuters reports Anthropic is aiming for a stock-market listing in mid-November at about $1.5tn, with its prospectus due "in coming days". Bloomberg reports OpenAI is raising about $30bn at a $1.25tn valuation. Both reports reached this brief through other outlets.

Brazil votes today, and the polls cannot split them

The world · sheet 5 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Brazil holds its presidential run-off today, Sunday. Flávio Bolsonaro faces President Lula.
  2. Datafolha's final poll on Saturday gave Flávio 51% and Lula 49% of valid votes. Datafolha calls it a technical tie.
  3. Quaest's final poll also had 51% to 49%. CNT/MDA had 50.7% to 49.3%.
  4. On Thursday Datafolha had the two level at 50% each.
  5. Flávio came first in the first round on 4 October, with about 47% to Lula's 45%.
  6. Investors see Flávio as the candidate of tighter budgets. Brazilian shares jumped after the first round.
Go deeper: a coin toss with a slight tilt

The polls. Rio Times counts Flávio ahead in 31 of 36 national run-off polls since the first round. AtlasIntel's poll for Bloomberg narrowed to 49.8% against 47.6%. Datafolha's raw totals, which include blank and undecided answers, were 48% to 47%. Prediction markets Polymarket and Kalshi gave Flávio 76–78% on Friday, Rio Times reported.

Congress. Flávio's Liberal Party became the largest party in the lower house on 4 October, with 121 of 513 seats, up from 97. Whoever wins the presidency will need its votes or its rivals' to pass laws.

Markets. On the Monday after the first round, a US fund that tracks Brazilian shares rose more than 12%, CNBC reported. Before the vote JPMorgan sketched two outcomes for the real: about 4.90 to the dollar if Flávio wins and about 5.50 if Lula wins. Public debt is above 82% of GDP.

America. Trump has not formally endorsed anyone, but he hosted Flávio at the White House. Lula's allies warn that America might not recognise a Lula win, NBC reported. Flávio is the son of Jair Bolsonaro, the former president, who was convicted last year.

Space and EO: a Russian satellite stalks a commercial radar satellite

Space and EO · sheet 6 of 10
ACosmos 2612 shadows ICEYE-X36
  1. A Russian military satellite, Cosmos 2612, closed in on ICEYE-X36, a commercial radar satellite.
  2. The tracking firm LeoLabs saw the gap shrink from 350 km to under 5 km by 16 October. Close passes went on until Sunday.
  3. The satellite is US-registered and Finnish-built. America has called it a satellite used by Ukraine for intelligence.
  4. On Wednesday America told a UN committee the approach was deliberate. An under-secretary called it threatening.
  5. On Thursday Vantor, formerly Maxar Intelligence, released its own picture of the Russian satellite near X36.
Go deeper: why one close pass matters to every EO operator

How close. Estimates differ. LeoLabs, with "high confidence", put the closest approach at about 500 metres, Sofrep reported. TWZ said it was possibly about 26 metres. Vantor imaged the pair about 1 km apart on 17 October. LeoLabs later saw the two "strongly diverging".

Why it is new. Russia has sent "inspector" satellites near US government satellites before, for example in 2020. The astronomer Jonathan McDowell told CNN this may be the first time one spacecraft approached another without permission. This time the target is a private, commercial Earth-observation satellite.

The two sides. ICEYE said it is "aware of the situation and tracking it closely", and that its "constellation architecture is designed for continuity and resilience". A Russian diplomat called the US accusations "absurd". LeoLabs said the aims could range from close inspection to counterspace preparation.

The lesson. Commercial imaging now has military value, so commercial satellites can become targets. Insurers, customers and governments buying data will ask how a fleet copes if one satellite is threatened. Many small satellites are harder to stop than one large one.

CICEYE launches four moreTuesday
  1. On Tuesday a Falcon 9 carried four ICEYE radar satellites on the Bandwagon-5 rideshare.
  2. Two are for Portugal's Atlantic Constellation. ICEYE did not say who owns the other two.
  3. ICEYE says it has now launched 84 radar satellites, more than any rival.
Go deeper: why Bandwagon orbits suit a defence customer

The orbit. Bandwagon flights go to mid-inclination orbits. These pass more often over mid-latitudes, such as Ukraine, the Middle East and Taiwan. Transporter flights go to sun-synchronous orbits, which cross the equator at the same local time each day.

The model. ICEYE sells whole satellites to governments, as with Poland and Portugal, and runs them in its fleet. Europe's governments are buying this "sovereign" model. The ICEYE satellite shadowed this week (panel A) is the case for many small satellites over a few large ones.

The agony of liberal Europe: riots in France, a tight Budget in Britain

The Economist's picks · sheet 7 of 10
AWhat happened

An AI proof keeps the zeta zeros away from the edge

The wonder piece · Quanta · sheet 8 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Monday Quanta reported that number theorists increasingly accept an AI-made proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis.
  2. The proof came in OpenAI's release of 377 maths results on 6 October, by Quanta's count.
  3. The Riemann hypothesis says all the important zeros of the zeta function lie on a single line.
  4. The weaker version asks only for a fixed strip, away from the edges, that holds every zero.
  5. A 199-page paper puts all the zeros between 1/8 and 7/8. A shorter, 49-page paper proves a wider strip, from 1/12 to 11/12.
  6. Since 1859, no one had proved that any fixed strip of this kind exists.
Go deeper: why a strip matters, and why the proof surprised experts

Why zeros count primes. The prime number theorem says roughly how many primes there are up to any number x. The zeros of zeta set the size of its error. Known zero-free regions all shrink toward the edge at 1, so the error shrinks more slowly than any power of x. A fixed strip with edge 7/8 caps the error at about x to the power 7/8.

What the proof does. Quanta says the proof "didn't need to introduce any new concepts". It joined old tools, some pioneered by Maksym Radziwiłł and Alexander Dunn, in a way experts had thought too weak. Dunn called it "a surprising synthesis of well-known ideas". After studying the 49-page paper, Radziwiłł could summarise its core argument in about half a page.

Why it matters. Radziwiłł called the implications "staggering": "In a sense, the vast majority of consequences of RH already follow from quasi-RH." Jared Duker Lichtman of Stanford called it "the most significant AI-for-math achievement so far, by quite a margin."

The checks. The 199-page paper was reported as checked in Lean. The 49-page paper, the one experts are reading, is the only release a human edited, and it is not Lean-checked. Peter Sarnak is using his course this semester to study the proof. He will not call it "a Theorem" until humans have checked every detail. Radziwiłł says both papers "fall short of the standard of exposition" in the field.

The process. OpenAI posted the papers on GitHub, not in a journal, and did not publish its prompts. Human work is already building on it: on arXiv, Baiying Liu improved the strip for a wider family of functions. Sam Altman said OpenAI's four biggest claims "have not yet been confirmed by outside mathematicians".

Science notebook: a softmax fix, AV2's date, and a warming verdict

Science · Nature and others · sheet 9 of 10
AMachine learning: when softmax gradients round to zeromachine learning
  1. On Thursday Jonathan Barron of Google DeepMind posted "A Fix for Softmax and Cross-Entropy" on arXiv.
  2. He names two failures. In "softmax collapse", the gradient 1 − p rounds to zero at high confidence.
  3. In "softmax cancellation", distillation stalls. The gradient is a difference of two nearly equal rounded numbers, so it never reaches zero.
  4. The fix computes the small quantities directly instead of by subtraction.
Go deeper: what it means for anyone training entropy models

The evidence. Barron reports that the fix removes the "Slingshot" effect, speeds up grokking under weight decay, and stops students drifting away from teachers. The experiments use small models. He says such extreme margins are uncommon in standard large-scale training. It is a single-author preprint, not yet peer reviewed.

Why compression people should care. Learned codecs train entropy models and codebook assignments with softmax, often in bf16. A probability model that becomes very confident hits the same cliff, and the rate term −log p is computed from the same small numbers. It is worth checking on an entropy-model training run.

The idea. The trick is old numerical practice, like computing log(1 − eˣ) with a dedicated function instead of subtracting. The paper's point is that the standard softmax and cross-entropy code paths do not do it.

The figure. The 30% is a BD-rate saving from the reference encoder, under standard test conditions, at high encoder effort. Real streaming gains usually start smaller, because production encoders must run fast.

History and places · sheet 10 of 10
AShaybah, Saudi Arabiaa place
  1. Shaybah is an oilfield on the northern edge of the Rub' al-Khali, the Empty Quarter.
  2. Aramco found it in 1968 but waited about 30 years to develop it. It needed too many wells to pay.
  3. Horizontal drilling changed that. Aramco approved the project in 1995, and oil began to flow in 1998.
  4. Today it can produce about 1 million barrels a day. On Wednesday drone debris killed two workers there (sheet 1).
Go deeper: an oil town among the dunes

Why so hard. The field lies deep in the desert, far from any town. By 1974 Aramco had drilled 30 test wells, but each vertical well produced too little. Horizontal wells, tested from 1993, raised the output of each well sharply and cut the number needed.

Growth. The first expansion took output to about 750,000 barrels a day by 2009. In 2016 Aramco added 250,000 more. It also built a gas-liquids plant and set up a 637 km² wildlife sanctuary next to the site.

This week. The Saudi energy ministry said the drone was aimed at a processing plant and that output was not affected. The two workers who died were from the Philippines and Nepal.
