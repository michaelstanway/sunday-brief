The week in 90 seconds

  1. Borrowing costs hit a 19-year high. America's 10-year Treasury yield closed the week near 5.2%, its highest since 2007, a week after the Fed's first rate rise since 2023. It's the story every source touched.
  2. Iran offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz; Trump said no. The plan would reopen the strait seven days after a deal. Trump called it "unacceptable" and renamed the waterway the "Trump Strait". EU diesel hit a record €2.23 a litre.
  3. Russia voted and Moscow was hit. Putin's party won a record 355 of 450 parliamentary seats while Ukraine launched its largest-ever drone attack on the capital.
  4. Germany's centre cracked. The far-right AfD won Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and Merz's CDU fell out of the state parliament entirely.
  5. Brazil votes next Sunday in a dead heat between Lula, 80, and Flávio Bolsonaro, son of a jailed ex-president.
  6. Planet will build satellites in Berlin, and the EU says it needs six times more launches by 2034. Europe is getting serious about its own space capacity.

Russia: a record election, and Moscow under attack

The world · sheet 2 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Russia held a parliamentary election from 18 to 20 September. It was the first since the 2022 invasion.
  2. Putin's United Russia won 355 of 450 seats in the Duma. That is a record.
  3. The only openly anti-war party, Yabloko, was kept off the ballot.
  4. On the last day, Ukraine launched its largest drone attack on Moscow. Russia said it stopped 1,600 drones, 450 aimed at the capital.
  5. The Moscow refinery stopped work after fires in both its main units. Three people died.
  6. Russia struck back on Thursday with missiles and 282 drones. At least 16 people died across Ukraine.

Is the universe a hologram?

Science · Quanta · sheet 7 of 10
AThe idea
  1. Draw a box around any part of space.
  2. The holographic principle says the box's surface can describe everything inside it.
  3. So a 3D world might be fully written on a 2D surface, like a hologram.
  4. This essay, by Charlie Wood in Quanta, asks what that means for reality.

DIn their words

I thought it was a little bit crazy, but I thought it was the least crazy of all the possibilities.Leonard Susskind, Stanford

We are fooled into thinking that there is more stuff in the universe than there actually is.Charles Cao, Virginia Tech

EWhat is still uncertain
  • Our universe expands and has no clear boundary. The exact maths may not apply.
  • Some read the black-hole results as ordinary entanglement, not holography.
  • Wood says physicists are "nowhere close" to a working theory, let alone evidence.

Science notebook

Science · Nature and The Economist · sheet 8 of 10
AVitamin D won't protect your bonesyou take this
  1. A BMJ review (published in May) pooled 69 trials and about 154,000 adults.
  2. Its advice: balance and strength training do more. This does not cover diagnosed deficiency.
BFake surgery often works as well
  1. A sham operation cuts the patient open but skips the key step.
  2. In a 10-year Finnish trial, real knee-cartilage surgery did worse than sham.
  3. Shoulder and some spine operations also failed against sham.
  4. The Economist: "millions of operations should be scrapped".
CEarth's core changes the length of your day

A Nature study found a tug-of-war inside the planet. Gravity wins, just. Days change by a few thousandths of a second over decades.

DWhy weight-loss jabs fail for some
  1. GLP-1 drugs cut about a quarter of body weight on average.
  2. But 10–15% of people lose little or none.
  3. Women tend to lose more. Oestrogen may help.
  4. One of the drugs' inventors says: "we really don't understand that."
EAn AI found a CRISPR-like system
  1. Anthropic's AI agents searched billions of gene sequences in 21 hours.
  2. They found CRISPR-like DNA in giant viruses.
  3. A Copenhagen group says it had studied this system for years. Credit is now in dispute.

Off the map: an empty island, and a library of ash

History and places · sheet 10 of 10
ASão Tomé and Príncipea place
  1. Two volcanic islands in the Gulf of Guinea, off Gabon. About 200,000 people live there.
  2. Nobody lived there until Portuguese sailors arrived on 21 December 1470.
  3. By 1905 it was the world's largest producer of cocoa. Cocoa is still its main crop.
  4. It became independent from Portugal, peacefully, in 1975.
  5. A tiny islet, Ilhéu das Rolas, sits on the Equator. It had 76 people at the 2012 census.
  6. The Economist visited this week: "What a beer with no label says about a small African country".
BThe scrolls of Herculaneuma piece of history
  1. In 79 AD, Vesuvius buried the Roman town of Herculaneum.
  2. One house, the Villa of the Papyri, held a library. More than 1,800 scrolls turned to charcoal.
  3. They were found in the 1700s. Most are too fragile to unroll.
  4. This week, researchers showed on replica scrolls that scans can read text from lead in the ink.
  5. It is the only complete library to survive from the ancient world.

Also this week

The world

  • Greenland: after a year of annexation threats, the US, Denmark and Greenland signed a defence deal at the UN. America gets new bases and overflight rights, non-NATO countries are shut out of military sites and sensitive investment, and Denmark keeps sovereignty. Al Jazeera ↗
  • China–US: during Xi Jinping's state visit, the two agreed tariff cuts on $30bn of goods, a new dialogue on AI with a hotline for "AI incidents", and a two-month extension of their trade truce. CNBC ↗
  • Britain: Prime Minister Andy Burnham, in office since July, sent an RAF tanker to refuel Saudi jets against Houthi attacks and launched a National Centre for Information Defence against AI-driven disinformation. Al Jazeera ↗
  • Venezuela: Trump met acting president Delcy Rodríguez, who took over after US forces captured Nicolás Maduro in January, and more political prisoners were released. Wikipedia ↗
  • Morocco voted on a 38% turnout. Fatima Ezzahra El Mansouri's party came first, putting her in line to be the country's first woman prime minister. Wikipedia ↗
  • Israel's election committee barred the main Arab parties from the coming election.
  • Weather: a record El Niño has left the Atlantic without a single hurricane this late in the season for the first time since 1914, while the Pacific produced a Category 5. Carbon Brief ↗

Money Stuff and the FT

  • "Who Cares If a Merger Is Fair?" Private equity firm Silver Lake sued Carl Icahn and dozens of hedge funds that bought into Endeavor after Silver Lake agreed to take it private at $27.50 a share, and are now asking a court to value the shares higher. Silver Lake calls them "opportunistic arbitrageurs", not genuine dissenters. About $4bn of shares are involved, reportedly Delaware's largest appraisal fight ever. Money Stuff ↗
  • "Kalshi Levers the Predictions." The prediction market Kalshi asked US regulators to let big traders bet on event contracts with borrowed money (margin), instead of putting up the full stake. Sports contracts are excluded; the earliest it could start is 9 November. Money Stuff ↗
  • "Morgan Stanley Leaked Some Deals." A Morgan Stanley staffer emailed out a file listing more than 100 Asian deals the bank is pitching, including IPO candidates and their backers. A recall email came too late, a blurred copy surfaced on Instagram, and rival banks started calling the companies on the list. Money Stuff ↗
  • From the FT's Lex column: "Big dreams and tiny revenue are new norm for AI IPOs", with Anthropic's expected listing the obvious example. FT ↗

The rest of The Economist

  • "When America walks away": decades of US dominance in the Middle East are ending. Iraq has set 30 September as the "fixed and final" date for the US-led coalition to leave. The Economist ↗
  • "Tata goes to war with itself": India's central bank says Tata Sons must list on the stock market. Its board voted 4–1 to move towards that and reappoint chairman N. Chandrasekaran; Noel Tata, whose charitable trusts own about 66% of the group, voted against and calls it void. It's heading to court. The Economist ↗
  • "German unions are fighting to defend the 35-hour week": IG Metall wants 5% pay rises while Mercedes threatens to close two plants unless it cuts €800m of labour costs. The Economist's view: the fight won't stop the car industry's decline. The Economist ↗
  • "Tanker rates are on the crest of a wave": with Hormuz mostly shut, a supertanker on the Gulf-to-China run earned about $723,000 a day, against $80,000 a year ago. The Economist ↗
  • "How tech firms hope to beat the backlash against 'pervert glasses'": after covert filming went viral, Meta launched a $349 pair of smart glasses with no camera at all. The Economist ↗
  • "Don't let AI kill the author": about 15% of British ministerial statements now contain AI-written passages, up from 3.5% in 2022, and research shows AI makes writing more uniform. The Economist ↗
  • "Data centres are straining the insurance market": about $10bn of new premiums this year, more risk than insurers can comfortably carry. The Economist ↗
  • Vitamin D and surgery: see Science below; both are worth your attention.
