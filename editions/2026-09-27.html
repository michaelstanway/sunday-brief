The world · about 6 minutes

The world

The Iran war: a deal offered, and refused

How we got here. The war began on 28 February, when the US and Israel struck Iran and killed its supreme leader. Iran fired back and shut the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow exit from the Gulf that normally carries about a fifth of the world's oil. A ceasefire in April led to failed talks in Islamabad, and America began blockading Iran's ports on 13 April. A second negotiating window in June collapsed on 8 July, and the blockade has been back in force since 14 July. Before the war more than 100 ships a day used the strait; in mid-September it was around 14.

Interactive figure: timeline

This week. On Tuesday, Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi spent about three hours with Trump's envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner at the UN in New York, through Qatari mediators. It was their first contact since June, and Iranian hard-liners were angry it happened at all. Iran then put forward a seven-day plan: reopen Hormuz a week after a deal, in exchange for ending the blockade, unfreezing about $12bn of assets, lifting oil sanctions and ending fighting on all fronts. Oil fell on the hope; US crude dropped nearly 8% on the week. On Saturday Trump rejected it as "unacceptable", saying Iran wanted a deal "because they're losing so badly", and posted an image renaming the waterway the "Trump Strait". The Wall Street Journal reported he privately expects to resume bombing after America's midterm elections on 3 November.

Interactive figure: hormuz

The cost. Iran's economy shrank 10.1% between March and June, and new US sanctions grounded its airlines internationally from 23 September. Europe pays at the pump: diesel averaged a record €2.23 a litre this week, up about 40% since February, because the Gulf supplied about 41% of Europe's diesel imports and Ukrainian drones have also cut Russian refining.

What to watch: Iran's formal reply through mediators, and whether "lift the blockade first" and "open the strait first" can be sequenced before 3 November.

Russia: a record election win, and Moscow under attack

The vote. In Russia's first parliamentary election since the 2022 invasion (18 to 20 September), Putin's United Russia won about 58% of the party-list vote and a record 355 of 450 seats, beating its 2016 high of 343. Yabloko, the only openly anti-war party, was kept off the ballot, and Finnish analysts called the official 59% turnout "implausibly high".

The attack. On the final day of voting, Ukraine launched what Moscow's mayor called the largest attack on the capital ever: more than 1,600 drones intercepted over Russia, about 450 of them aimed at Moscow. Three people died, more than 150 flights were cancelled or delayed, and the Moscow refinery, which supplies about 40% of the city's fuel, stopped processing after both its distillation units caught fire. The mayor said it was "clearly planned with the aim of disrupting the elections". Russia answered on Thursday with Zircon and Iskander missiles and 282 drones, killing at least 16 people across Ukraine.

Testing Europe. On Wednesday a Russian helicopter flew about 300 metres into Polish airspace for 42 seconds; Poland scrambled jets and said Russia was "once again testing the readiness of our air defence". It follows a summer of what The Economist calls "grey-zone" attacks (sabotage, arson, and a drone strike at Germany's Leipzig airport), which it argues are "growing more brazen". At the UN, Germany's and Russia's foreign ministers met for the first time since 2022. Lavrov said "nothing new came from that initiative".

What to watch: Russia's promised retaliation for the Moscow strikes, how long the refinery stays down, and winter attacks on Ukraine's power grid. The Institute for the Study of War expects Putin to lean on long-range strikes this winter.

Ethiopia: the makings of a second Tigray war

Background. Ethiopia's 2020–22 war between the federal government and the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF) is estimated to have killed hundreds of thousands. It ended with the Pretoria agreement in November 2022, but the peace frayed: the TPLF split, relations with neighbouring Eritrea soured, and since 2023 the Fano militia has fought the government in the Amhara region.

This week. Last Sunday seven armed groups, including the TPLF, Fano, the Oromo Liberation Army and the Ogaden National Liberation Front, formed a coalition and pledged to "overthrow the Abiy Ahmed-led government" and install a transitional administration. More than 20 government air strikes on Alamata, in southern Tigray, killed 14 civilians and hit two empty schools. The TPLF called it an "open invasion", declared a "defensive war" and seized the airports at Mekelle, Axum and Shire; flights were suspended, and the African Union said this broke the Pretoria deal. Fighting opened on three fronts, and a Tigrayan leader accused Eritrea and Egypt of backing armed groups.

Why it matters: a war across several regions at once, with neighbours drawn in, could be worse than the last one, in a country of about 130 million. One analyst calls the alliance "tactical rather than strategic", so it may not hold together.

Markets and money · about 5 minutes

Markets and money

The bond market flinched, and house prices are next

What happened. America's 10-year Treasury yield, the benchmark rate that sets mortgage costs around the world, climbed about 0.19 percentage points this week and closed Friday near 5.2%, its highest since July 2007. A weak auction of five-year government bonds on Wednesday showed buyers demanding more to lend to Washington. Behind it: oil above $100 since March, inflation that won't settle, big government deficits, and a central bank that has started raising rates again.

Interactive figure: seesaw

The central banks. On 16 September the Federal Reserve, now chaired by Kevin Warsh, raised rates by a quarter point to 3.75–4.00%, its first rise since July 2023, unanimously. Sixteen of its eighteen policymakers expect another rise this year, and it now sees inflation at 3.7% this year and back at 2% only in 2029. The ECB raised its deposit rate to 2.50% on 10 September. The Bank of England held at 3.75%, but three of nine members wanted a rise, and Governor Andrew Bailey warned "the longer this volatility persists… the more likely it is we will need to raise Bank rate." Stocks shrugged it off: the S&P 500 still rose 1.2% on the week to 7,743, and Meta jumped about 13% around its smart-glasses launch.

Why it matters for houses. The Economist's leader this week asks whether house prices can survive. They survived the last rate rise, it argues, but this time the supports are gone, with only "crumbs of comfort". Those supports were Americans locked into 3–4% fixed mortgages who didn't sell, and Britons on fixed deals that reset slowly. Now America's 30-year mortgage rate is back above 7%, there are 4.9 months of homes for sale, the most in over a decade, and Britain's typical two-year fix is around 5.7%, with UK prices already slipping 0.4% on the year. The comfort: US prices are still up 1.6%, and many owners have large equity cushions.

Interactive figure: mortgage

What to watch: whether the 10-year yield holds above 5%, the Bank of England's next decision on 5 November, and whether the Fed hikes again. For anyone with a UK fixed-rate mortgage ending soon, this is the week remortgaging got more expensive.

The Economist's picks

Two of this week's cover stories explained, then the rest of the issue in a line each. The italic lines are The Economist's own argument, from its headlines.

Brazil: "a vengeful nepo-baby or a doddering leftie"

The Economist: Brazil is turning its back on the future, whichever of two unappealing candidates wins.

The candidates. President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, 80, is seeking a fourth term. His challenger is Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, 44, eldest son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, who is in prison serving 27 years for a coup plot and endorsed Flávio last December. The first round is next Sunday, 4 October, and a runoff on 25 October is likely.

The polls. It's a coin toss. Datafolha has Lula ahead 40% to 36% in the first round and 47% to 45% in a runoff; Quaest has Flávio a point ahead in a runoff. Evangelicals favour Flávio about two to one. For the first time this term, more Brazilians disapprove of Lula (51%) than approve.

Interactive figure: polls

The issues. Crime is voters' top worry, with gangs like Comando Vermelho and the PCC, and Flávio wants to lower the age of criminal responsibility to 16. Corruption comes next: the collapse of Banco Master, described as Brazil's biggest banking fraud, led to its owner's arrest, and a Supreme Court justice is investigating whether about $10m–12m of its money funded a biopic of Jair Bolsonaro. Flávio's answer: "Investigate everything and everyone." Government debt is about 83% of GDP, and US tariffs have let Lula campaign on national "sovereignty". On Thursday Lula banned online betting by decree; Brazilians stake about R$60bn a year, three-quarters of voters support a ban, and Flávio called it "populist, hypocritical and politically motivated".

What to watch: whether either candidate clears 50% next Sunday (unlikely), and where voters for the minor candidates go in the runoff on 25 October.

Friedrich Merz, the zombie chancellor

The Economist: Merz cannot recover, but no one wants to finish him off, yet.

The results. In Mecklenburg-Vorpommern the AfD won 38.2% and came first. Merz's CDU got 4.9%, below the 5% threshold, so it won no seats at all, the first time since reunification it's been shut out of a state parliament. In Berlin the hard-left Die Linke came first with 25.7%, with the CDU second and the AfD third. Two weeks earlier the AfD took 43.8% in Saxony-Anhalt. Other parties refuse to govern with the AfD, so both states will likely end up with left-leaning coalitions.

Interactive figure: seats

The fallout. "We cannot sugar-coat the result. It is a disaster," Merz said, while insisting "the reforms have to come." His coalition partner, the SPD, now wants to reopen his plans on pensions, care and the 2027 budget. Its leader Lars Klingbeil said that if people see reforms as a threat, "it is not just our communication that needs to improve, but… the substance of the reform itself." Nationally the AfD polls about 28–29% to the CDU's 20%, and in one poll only 14% of Germans think Merz is the right chancellor. The Economist sees a Europe-wide pattern: populists on the right and the left gaining as the centre weakens.

What to watch: coalition talks in both states, whether the SPD forces a rewrite of the 2027 budget, and whether CDU figures like Hendrik Wüst or Markus Söder start openly positioning to replace Merz.

Science · about 6 minutes

Science

Is the universe a hologram? Quanta

This week's Quanta piece, by Charlie Wood, is an essay rather than news: a guide to one of the strangest ideas in physics and what it might mean for reality.

The idea. Draw a box around any region of space. The holographic principle says everything inside could, in principle, be described completely by information on the box's surface. That means a three-dimensional world might be fully encoded in two dimensions, the way a flat hologram stores a 3D image.

Interactive figure: holo

Why physicists take it seriously. First, gravity is unusual: mass is always positive, so the way a region's surface bends space-time can pin down what's inside, in a way electric charge can't. Second, in the 1970s Jacob Bekenstein and Stephen Hawking found that the information a black hole holds grows with its surface area, not its volume. Since any region could in principle collapse into a black hole, every region may follow the same rule. Third, in the late 1990s physicists found an exact mathematical match: a universe with gravity, shaped like a closed "snow globe", is equivalent to a theory with no gravity at all living on its boundary. That result, called AdS/CFT, is among the most-cited ideas in modern theoretical physics. Leonard Susskind remembers thinking the holographic idea "was a little bit crazy, but I thought it was the least crazy of all the possibilities."

The catch. Our universe isn't a snow globe. It's expanding and has no obvious boundary, so nobody knows whether the exact maths applies to us. Some physicists read the black-hole results more mundanely, as ordinary quantum entanglement. Vijay Balasubramanian lays out three options: the surface description is the real one, the 3D world with gravity is real, or neither is and both are drafts of a theory we don't have yet. As Charles Cao puts it: "We are fooled into thinking that there is more stuff in the universe than there actually is." Wood's own conclusion is that physicists are "nowhere close" to a working theory for our universe, let alone evidence.

Vitamin D won't protect your bones you take this

Interactive figure: predict

The Economist highlighted a big BMJ review (published in May) of 69 trials and about 154,000 adults. Vitamin D on its own had no effect on fractures, with high certainty. Vitamin D plus calcium cut fractures by about 9%, below the threshold the authors had set in advance as meaningful. They conclude the evidence does "not support routine supplementation" for fractures and falls, and suggest balance and strength training instead. It doesn't cover diagnosed deficiency, and NHS advice to consider 10 micrograms a day from October to March still stands.

Why weight-loss jabs don't work for everyone Nature

Most people on GLP-1 drugs like semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) lose a lot of weight, about a quarter of body weight on average in one study, but 10–15% lose little or none. Daniel Drucker, whose research helped create the drugs, says "we really don't understand that." There are clues: women tend to lose more than men, and two studies found post-menopausal women on hormone therapy did better, which hints that oestrogen matters. Data on genetic differences is still thin, and the Endocrine Society calls truly personalised obesity treatment "largely aspirational".

Earth's core is changing the length of your day Nature

Days get longer or shorter by a few thousandths of a second over decades. A study in Nature this week, by Mathieu Dumberry and Huifeng Zhang at the University of Alberta, explains why: a tug-of-war inside the planet. Gravity pulls dense parts of the inner core towards dense parts of the mantle, while magnetic and frictional forces pull the other way. Gravity wins, just. It fits earlier findings that the inner core spun faster than the rest of the planet until about 2010, then slowed.

Anthropic's AI found a CRISPR-like system in giant viruses Nature

Anthropic, the company behind Claude, opened a biology lab and released a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed. In it, about 950 AI agents spent 21 hours searching billions of gene sequences and flagged repeating DNA patterns in giant viruses that resemble CRISPR, the bacterial immune system behind gene editing. What the system does is unknown; Anthropic's head of life sciences calls it "a promising lead". A twist: a Copenhagen researcher says his group had studied this system for years and uploaded information about it to Anthropic's models. Anthropic says the model was "not trained on any user transcripts". It's a live test of whether AI can make discoveries, and how to credit them.

Space and EO · about 2 minutes

Space and Earth observation

  • Planet opens a satellite factory in Berlin (22 Sep). A 5,700 m² facility, reportedly able to build up to 60 satellites a year, with its first German-built Pelican due before year-end and a launch on Isar Aerospace's Spectrum rocket from Norway in early 2027. Why it matters for EO: one of the biggest operators is scaling up its fleet, which means more imagery to get down every day, and it's buying European manufacturing and launch. Via Satellite ↗
  • The EU wants six times more launches by 2034 (23 to 26 Sep). Space commissioner Andrius Kubilius said EU government satellites will rise from about 70 to about 630, needing about 62 launches a year, and Ariane 6 alone would need 14 a year by 2031 against 9 or 10 now. The FT reports the EU is looking at launch sites in Norway, the UK and Canada. Why it matters: launch supply, not just satellites, is now the bottleneck for new European EO constellations. Eunews ↗
  • SSC becomes ground-station partner for Airbus's Pléiades Neo (21 Sep), handling data downlink through stations in Sweden, Canada and Chile, with "latency optimisation", including for the coming 20 cm-class Pléiades Neo Next in 2028. Sharper images mean more data per image, and downlink is being bought as an optimised service. SSC ↗
  • China launched "Supercomputing-1" (20 Sep), an imaging satellite with an onboard AI computer meant to process EO data in orbit, cutting processing from hours to minutes. Details are not independently confirmed. Tom's Hardware ↗
  • Starship's 14th flight slipped to 28 September pending regulatory approval. The Space Force launched another batch of satellites from Vandenberg on 26 September.
  • Earlier in the month: Vantor (formerly Maxar) ordered a 24-satellite imaging constellation with revisits every 15 minutes, EnduroSat raised $205m, and ESA picked ICEYE- and Leonardo-led teams to design Europe's government EO service. September's European space funding passed €1bn. SpaceNews ↗
