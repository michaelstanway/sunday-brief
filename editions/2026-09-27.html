The week in 90 seconds

  1. Borrowing costs hit a 19-year high. America's 10-year Treasury yield closed the week near 5.2%, its highest since 2007, a week after the Fed's first rate rise since 2023. It's the story every source touched.
  2. Iran offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz; Trump said no. The plan would reopen the strait seven days after a deal. Trump called it "unacceptable" and renamed the waterway the "Trump Strait". EU diesel hit a record €2.23 a litre.
  3. Russia voted and Moscow was hit. Putin's party won a record 355 of 450 parliamentary seats while Ukraine launched its largest-ever drone attack on the capital.
  4. Germany's centre cracked. The far-right AfD won Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and Merz's CDU fell out of the state parliament entirely.
  5. Brazil votes next Sunday in a dead heat between Lula, 80, and Flávio Bolsonaro, son of a jailed ex-president.
  6. Planet will build satellites in Berlin, and the EU says it needs six times more launches by 2034. Europe is getting serious about its own space capacity.

The Iran war: a deal offered, and refused

The world · sheet 1 of 10
AWhat happenedSun 20 – Sat 26 Sep
  1. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, met Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner at the UN on Tuesday. It was the first contact since June.
  2. Iran then offered a seven-day plan. It would reopen the Strait of Hormuz seven days after a deal.
  3. In return, America must end its blockade, unfreeze about $12bn and lift oil sanctions.
  4. On Saturday Trump called the plan "unacceptable". He posted an image that renamed the waterway the "Trump Strait".
  5. The Wall Street Journal reported that he expects to resume bombing after the US midterms on 3 November.
Go deeper: how the war got here and what it costs

The road here. The war began on 28 February, when US and Israeli strikes killed Iran's supreme leader. Iran answered with missiles and drones and closed the Strait of Hormuz. A ceasefire in April led to talks in Islamabad, which failed. America then blockaded Iran's ports from 13 April. A June memorandum opened 60 days of talks, but the ceasefire collapsed on 8 July, and the blockade returned on 14 July with new US strikes. US Central Command says it has turned back 115 commercial ships since.

The meeting. The talks at the UN lasted about three hours and ran through Qatari mediators. Iranian hard-liners were angry that they happened at all. Iran's plan had four conditions: end the blockade, release about $12bn of frozen assets, lift oil sanctions, and end fighting on all fronts.

The squeeze on Iran. Its statistics office says the economy shrank 10.1% from March to June, with oil and gas output down about a quarter. The IMF expects a 5.4% fall for the year. America's "Economic D-Day" sanctions grounded 27 Iranian airlines abroad from 23 September. Turkish Airlines suspended Iran flights until March 2027. Schools and universities reopened this week, with 16.5 million children back in class.

The cost to Europe. The Middle East supplied about 41% of Europe's diesel imports in 2025. Gulf diesel exports have halved, and Ukrainian drones have cut Russian refining, so Russia banned diesel exports in July. EU diesel averaged a record €2.23 a litre, up about 40% since February. Nineteen countries set records; the Netherlands passed €2.57.

Russia: a record election, and Moscow under attack

The world · sheet 2 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Russia held a parliamentary election from 18 to 20 September. It was the first since the 2022 invasion.
  2. Putin's United Russia won 355 of 450 seats in the Duma. That is a record.
  3. The only openly anti-war party, Yabloko, was kept off the ballot.
  4. On the last day, Ukraine launched its largest drone attack on Moscow. Russia said it stopped 1,600 drones, 450 aimed at the capital.
  5. The Moscow refinery stopped work after fires in both its main units. Three people died.
  6. Russia struck back on Thursday with missiles and 282 drones. At least 16 people died across Ukraine.
Go deeper: the election and the attack in detail

The vote. United Russia took about 58% of the party-list vote. Its 355 seats beat its 2016 record of 343. The Communists won 40 seats and the LDPR 25. Finnish analysts at FIIA called the official 59% turnout "implausibly high". Russia's election chief blamed more than 1,000 waves of cyber-attacks.

The attack. Moscow's mayor, Sergei Sobyanin, called it the largest attack on the capital ever. A drone hit a 21-storey block, and 400 people were evacuated. More than 150 flights at Sheremetyevo were cancelled or delayed. The Moscow refinery supplies about 40% of the city's fuel; repairs could take weeks. Ukraine hit a second refinery, in Krasnodar, later in the week.

Russia's reply. On Thursday Russia fired Zircon and Iskander missiles and 282 drones, mainly at Kyiv and Odesa. At least 16 people died across Ukraine and a maternity hospital was damaged. On Friday another strike killed seven in Kyiv, including a 14-year-old.

Diplomacy. Germany's Johann Wadephul met Lavrov for about 20 minutes at the UN and urged Russia off its "dangerous path of escalation". Poland's and Belarus's foreign ministers also met, for the first time in a decade. The Institute for the Study of War expects Putin to rely on long-range strikes this winter.

Ethiopia: the makings of a second Tigray war

The world · sheet 3 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Ethiopia's last war, from 2020 to 2022, killed hundreds of thousands. The Pretoria agreement ended it.
  2. Last Sunday, seven armed groups formed an alliance. They want to overthrow the government of Abiy Ahmed.
  3. Government air strikes on Alamata, in Tigray, killed 14 civilians.
  4. The TPLF then declared a "defensive war". It took the airports at Mekelle, Axum and Shire.
  5. The African Union said this broke the Pretoria deal.
Go deeper: the background to the war

The 2020–22 war was between the federal government and the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF). Estimates of the dead run into the hundreds of thousands. The Pretoria agreement of November 2022 ended it.

The peace then frayed. The TPLF split. Relations with neighbouring Eritrea soured. Since 2023 the Fano militia has fought the government in Amhara.

This week's alliance is called the Ethiopian Peoples' Forces Coalition for Survival. More than 20 government air strikes on Alamata hit two empty schools. After the TPLF took three airports, flights were suspended and fighting opened on three fronts. One analyst, Jawar Mohammed, called the alliance "tactical rather than strategic".

The bond market flinched, and house prices are next

Markets and money · sheet 4 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. America's 10-year Treasury yield closed near 5.2%. That is its highest since 2007.
  2. The causes: oil above $100, inflation that will not settle, big deficits and a weak bond auction on Wednesday.
  3. On 16 September the Fed, led by Kevin Warsh, raised rates to 3.75–4.00%. It was its first rise since 2023.
  4. The ECB raised rates on 10 September. The Bank of England, under Andrew Bailey, held. But 3 of its 9 members wanted a rise.
  5. Stocks did not panic. The S&P 500 rose 1.2% on the week.
Go deeper: the central banks and the bond market

The Fed. The vote on 16 September was unanimous. Sixteen of the 18 policymakers expect at least one more rise this year. The Fed now expects 3.7% inflation this year and a return to 2% only in 2029. Kevin Warsh's argument: the Fed cannot set the price of oil, but it can stop a jump in fuel costs spreading to everything else.

Europe. The ECB raised its deposit rate to 2.50%, saying the Middle East conflict will keep inflation "well above target". The Bank of England held at 3.75%. UK inflation is 3.1% and expected to pass 4% early next year, mostly because of fuel. Andrew Bailey warned: "the longer this volatility persists… the more likely it is we will need to raise Bank rate." Britain's 10-year gilt yields about 5.2%.

Housing. In 2022–23, US owners with 3–4% fixed mortgages simply did not sell, so supply shrank as fast as demand. In Britain, most loans are fixed for two to five years, so higher rates arrived slowly. Both supports are wearing out. US existing home sales fell 2% in August. The US median sale price was still up 1.6% on the year, at $429,100.

Brazil: "a vengeful nepo-baby or a doddering leftie"

The Economist's picks · sheet 5 of 10
AWhat happened

Brazil is turning its back on the future, whichever of two weak candidates wins.The Economist, 26 September

  1. Brazil votes on 4 October. A run-off on 25 October is likely.
  2. President Lula, 80, wants a fourth term.
  3. His rival is Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, 44. His father, Jair Bolsonaro, is in prison for a coup plot.
  4. On Thursday Lula banned online betting by decree. Flávio called it "populist".
Go deeper: the issues and the scandal

Crime is voters' top worry. Gangs such as Comando Vermelho and the PCC are powerful. Flávio Bolsonaro wants to lower the age of criminal responsibility to 16.

Corruption comes next. The collapse of Banco Master is called Brazil's biggest banking fraud; its owner, Daniel Vorcaro, was arrested. A Supreme Court justice is investigating whether about $10m–12m of Master money funded a biopic of Jair Bolsonaro. Flávio's answer: "Investigate everything and everyone."

The economy. Government debt is about 83% of GDP and growth is modest. US tariffs have let Lula campaign on national "sovereignty".

The betting ban. Brazilians stake about R$60bn a year online. About three-quarters of the public support a ban. Users have until 5 October to withdraw funds, and Congress must approve the decree within 120 days.

Friedrich Merz, the zombie chancellor

The Economist's picks · sheet 6 of 10
AWhat happened

Merz cannot recover, but no one wants to finish him off, yet.The Economist, 26 September

  1. Two German states voted last Sunday.
  2. In Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, the far-right AfD came first with 38.2%.
  3. Merz's CDU got 4.9%. That is under the 5% threshold, so it won no seats.
  4. In Berlin, the hard-left Die Linke came first.
  5. Merz said: "It is a disaster." His partner, the SPD, now wants to change his reforms.
Go deeper: what the results mean for Merz

Turnout in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern was 78%. The CDU's 4.9% is its worst post-war state result. Two weeks earlier, the AfD won 43.8% in Saxony-Anhalt.

In Berlin, Die Linke won 25.7%, the CDU 18.8%, the AfD 16.3% and the SPD 12.1%, its worst result ever.

Merz insisted that "the reforms have to come." The SPD's leader, Lars Klingbeil, replied that if people see reforms as a threat, "it is not just our communication that needs to improve, but… the substance of the reform itself." In one poll only 14% of Germans think Merz is the right chancellor.

Is the universe a hologram?

Science · Quanta · sheet 7 of 10
AThe idea
  1. Draw a box around any part of space.
  2. The holographic principle says the box's surface can describe everything inside it.
  3. So a 3D world might be fully written on a 2D surface, like a hologram.
  4. This essay, by Charlie Wood in Quanta, asks what that means for reality.
Go deeper: the three clues, explained

Gravity. Mass is always positive. So the way a region's surface bends space-time can pin down one arrangement of matter inside. Electric charges can cancel, so a surface cannot do the same for them. Laurent Freidel of the Perimeter Institute: "Intuitively, this is why holography is plausible."

Black holes. In the 1970s, Jacob Bekenstein and Stephen Hawking found that a black hole's entropy, a measure of its information, grows with its surface area, not its volume. Leonard Susskind argued in the 1990s that a black hole is literally a hologram. Any region of space could collapse into a black hole, so every region may obey the same limit.

AdS/CFT. In the late 1990s, physicists found an exact match. A universe with gravity, curved like a closed "snow globe", is mathematically the same as a gravity-free quantum theory on its boundary. The papers are among the most cited in modern theoretical physics.

The doubts. Our universe is expanding and has no boundary. Latham Boyle of Edinburgh reads the black-hole results as ordinary entanglement, "a less mystical, more down-to-earth interpretation". Vijay Balasubramanian of Penn sees three options: the surface is real, the volume is real, or neither is.

Science notebook

Science · Nature and The Economist · sheet 8 of 10
AVitamin D won't protect your bonesyou take this
  1. A BMJ review (published in May) pooled 69 trials and about 154,000 adults.
  2. Its advice: balance and strength training do more. This does not cover diagnosed deficiency.
Go deeper: the evidence on vitamin D

The review. Canadian researchers led by Olivier Massé pooled 69 randomised trials and 153,902 adults. Most were healthy and lived at home. For vitamin D alone, the risk of any fracture was unchanged: a relative risk of 1.00 across 36 trials and 92,045 people, rated high certainty. Vitamin D with calcium gave a relative risk of 0.91, about 9% fewer fractures, but below the authors' pre-set threshold for a meaningful benefit.

It matches earlier trials. VITAL gave nearly 26,000 Americans 2,000 IU a day and found no fracture benefit. Australia's D-Health trial gave 21,315 people 60,000 IU a month and found the same. In 2024 the Endocrine Society advised against routine supplements for healthy adults under 75.

Who it does not cover. People with diagnosed deficiency, bone disease or on osteoporosis drugs, and care-home residents, were few in the trials. NHS advice still says everyone in the UK should consider 10 micrograms (400 IU) a day from October to March, and year-round for people who rarely go outside or have darker skin.

The practical reading. If you take vitamin D for your bones, the evidence says it is doing little. If a blood test showed you are low, that is a separate case for a doctor. The authors point to balance and strength training to prevent falls and fractures.

BFake surgery often works as well
  1. A sham operation cuts the patient open but skips the key step.
  2. In a 10-year Finnish trial, real knee-cartilage surgery did worse than sham.
  3. Shoulder and some spine operations also failed against sham.
  4. The Economist: "millions of operations should be scrapped".
Go deeper: the trials behind the claim

Knees. Finland's FIDELITY trial gave 146 patients with a worn meniscus either partial removal or a sham. After 10 years, published in May, the surgery group had more symptoms and faster arthritis. More than half a million of these operations are done in America each year.

Shoulders. Britain's CSAW trial found that shaving bone to relieve impingement did no better than a placebo arthroscopy. Two repairs for a common shoulder tear also failed to beat sham.

Spines. Cement injections into fractured vertebrae failed against sham in 2009. A 2024 Scandinavian trial of sacroiliac-joint fusion found no difference at six months.

Across surgery. A review of 53 placebo-controlled surgical trials found the sham did as well as the real operation in more than half. Procedures, unlike drugs, face no regulator's test that they work, and surgeons are often paid per operation. A useful question before any procedure: has it ever beaten a sham?

CEarth's core changes the length of your day

A Nature study found a tug-of-war inside the planet. Gravity wins, just. Days change by a few thousandths of a second over decades.

DWhy weight-loss jabs fail for some
  1. GLP-1 drugs cut about a quarter of body weight on average.
  2. But 10–15% of people lose little or none.
  3. Women tend to lose more. Oestrogen may help.
  4. One of the drugs' inventors, Daniel Drucker, says: "we really don't understand that."
EAn AI found a CRISPR-like system
  1. Anthropic's AI agents searched billions of gene sequences in 21 hours.
  2. They found CRISPR-like DNA in giant viruses.
  3. A Copenhagen group says it had studied this system for years. Credit is now in dispute.

Space and Earth observation

Space and EO · sheet 9 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Planet opened a satellite factory in Berlin. It may build up to 60 satellites a year. Its first will fly on Isar's Spectrum rocket from Norway in early 2027.
  2. The EU's space commissioner, Andrius Kubilius, says it needs six times more launches by 2034. It is looking at sites in Norway, the UK and Canada.
  3. SSC will run downlink for Airbus's Pléiades Neo satellites, including a sharper 20 cm class from 2028.
  4. China launched a satellite with an onboard AI computer to process images in orbit. Details are not confirmed.
Go deeper: the details for EO

Planet. The Berlin site is 5,700 m². Planet plans 70 new hires there on top of about 150. Its first German-built Pelican is due before the end of 2026, and the Isar launch is booked for early 2027 from Andøya, Norway.

Europe's launch gap. Andrius Kubilius said EU government satellites will rise from about 70 to about 630, and national defence satellites from about 20 to about 300. That needs about 62 launches a year by 2034, a sixfold rise.

SSC and Airbus. SSC will handle telemetry and high-volume image downlink through stations in Sweden (Esrange), Canada (Inuvik) and Chile (Punta Arenas).

Earlier this month. Vantor (formerly Maxar) ordered a 24-satellite constellation that can revisit a site every 15 minutes. EnduroSat raised $205m. ESA chose ICEYE- and Leonardo-led teams to design Europe's government EO service. European space funding passed €1bn in September.

Off the map: an empty island, and a library of ash

History and places · sheet 10 of 10
ASão Tomé and Príncipea place
  1. Two volcanic islands in the Gulf of Guinea, off Gabon. About 200,000 people live there.
  2. Nobody lived there until Portuguese sailors arrived on 21 December 1470.
  3. By 1905 it was the world's largest producer of cocoa. Cocoa is still its main crop.
  4. It became independent from Portugal, peacefully, in 1975.
  5. A tiny islet, Ilhéu das Rolas, sits on the Equator. It had 76 people at the 2012 census.
  6. The Economist visited this week: "What a beer with no label says about a small African country".
BThe scrolls of Herculaneuma piece of history
  1. In 79 AD, Vesuvius buried the Roman town of Herculaneum.
  2. One house, the Villa of the Papyri, held a library. More than 1,800 scrolls turned to charcoal.
  3. They were found in the 1700s. Most are too fragile to unroll.
  4. This week, researchers showed on replica scrolls that scans can read text from lead in the ink.
  5. It is the only complete library to survive from the ancient world.
