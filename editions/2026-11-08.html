The week a blue wave swept America

Democrats took Congress in the biggest midterm wave since Watergate, and markets swung from relief to fear for the AI build-out. Oil fell on bets that the Iran war will end sooner, Israel's president turned to Eisenkot, the Bank of England raised rates, and California picked a new governor while Los Angeles ousted its mayor. The week in 90 seconds

  1. America's voters delivered the biggest wave since Watergate. Democrats won the House and the Senate on Tuesday, and flipped five governorships in states Trump won. Vance blamed the war.
  2. The AI trade met the ballot box. Fear that new Democratic governors will pause data centres knocked 4.4% off the S&P 500 on Thursday. Nvidia fell 9% and AMD 17%.
  3. Oil had its biggest weekly fall since 2020. Brent fell to about $98 as traders bet a Democratic Congress will hasten the end of the Iran war, though Hormuz stays contested.
  4. Israel's president gave Gadi Eisenkot the first try at a government. Parties with 67 seats backed him, but 19 of those seats belong to Arab-led parties he will not govern with.
  5. The Bank of England raised rates for the first time since 2023. Bank Rate rose to 4% on an 8–1 vote, and the Bank sees inflation near 5% next year.
  6. California chose Xavier Becerra as governor, and Los Angeles ousted its mayor. Nithya Raman beat Karen Bass, and a tax on billionaires' wealth failed while a rival measure passed.

America's blue wave: the biggest since Watergate

America · sheet 1 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Tuesday Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate. AP called the Senate for them at 11:37pm Eastern time.
  2. Republicans went in holding the Senate 53–47. CNN and ABC put Democrats on 54 seats, with a few races still open.
  3. In the House, Democrats won more than 250 seats, CNN reported. A majority is 218.
  4. In Texas, James Talarico beat Ken Paxton by about 12 points. It is the first Democratic Senate win in Texas since 1988.
  5. Democrats also flipped five governorships in states Trump won in 2024: Georgia, Iowa, Nevada, Ohio and Texas.
  6. On Wednesday JD Vance wrote that "the reason we lost is the war". On Saturday Donald Trump said he had told the Justice Department to look into Democrats who threaten to punish his officials.
Go deeper: the seats, the leaders and the ballot measures

The Senate. Reported Democratic gains include Iowa, where Josh Turek beat Ashley Hinson, Kansas, where Adam Hamilton ousted Roger Marshall, and North Carolina, where Roy Cooper won. Ohio, Maine and South Carolina were also reported as flips. Jon Ossoff held Georgia.

The House. A running tally by MS NOW had Democrats on 256 and Republicans on 170, with races still uncalled. Speaker Mike Johnson said on Wednesday he would not run for minority leader. Hakeem Jeffries is set to become Speaker in January, the first Black Speaker.

The voters. An Economist/YouGov poll taken Wednesday to Friday found independents broke nearly two to one for Democratic House candidates. ABC's voter poll found about a third called the war "crucial" to their vote. About half said the same of the cost of living or corruption.

The ballot measures. Abortion rights won in three of four states, NPR reported. Local votes on data centres went almost all one way: in one tally, 37 of 38 counted measures went against them. Sheet 2 explains why that shook markets.

Trump's response. On election night Trump told the New York Times: "I am absolutely willing to accept those results." Newsweek reported that he later reposted material questioning the Democratic vote totals.

BHow it workstwo sliders

In a House election, a lead in votes is turned into a lead in seats. The rule of thumb once called the cube law says seats swing three times as hard as votes. But when few districts are competitive, seats respond less. The Economist's podcast reported a Democratic lead of about 13 points in the national House vote, the largest in a midterm since 1974. Move the sliders to see how many seats such a lead buys.

CThe argument

Donald Trump's humiliation offers America an opportunityThe Economist, cover leader, 5 November

The Economist's view. It calls this "the biggest wave-election since the aftermath of Watergate more than 50 years ago", with upsets in red states "from South Carolina to Texas". Its reading, as reported by others: voters will turn on parties they have long backed. Both parties should take the hint. A separate Economist chart piece says moderate Democrats, not progressives, won most of the hard seats.

The strongest counter-view. Midterms usually punish the president's party, so a wave is a reaction, not a realignment. Divided government tends to bring investigations and vetoes, not renewal. Trump's order to the Justice Department on Saturday points to confrontation.

DWhat to watch
  • The leadership races. If Republicans pick a combative House leader such as Jim Jordan, expect a fight over every investigation; a quieter choice would suggest deal-making on spending.
  • January's handover. With the House gavel, Democrats gain subpoena power. The first targets will show whether they choose oversight or a war with the White House.
  • Iran. Congress can vote to limit the war. A vote that passes both chambers would test Trump's veto and move oil prices (sheet 3).

The AI trade meets the voters

Markets · sheet 2 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Wednesday the Dow rose 3.03%, its best day since April 2025. Investors bet that a Democratic Congress makes an end to the Iran war more likely.
  2. On Thursday the mood flipped. The S&P 500 fell 4.36% and the Nasdaq 5.52%, its worst day in about 18 months. CNN put the day's losses at about $2.5trn.
  3. Chipmakers fell hardest. Nvidia lost 9.3%. AMD lost 17.3%, two days after record results.
  4. The fear was local. Democrats won governorships in states that host many data centres, and local votes went against new ones almost everywhere.
  5. Goldman Sachs wrote that Democratic wins "are likely to lead to short-term data center moratoria". It estimates that about 45% of planned US capacity growth could be affected.
  6. Chips bounced on Friday. Over the week the S&P 500 fell 2.68% and the Nasdaq 3.37%.
Go deeper: why a local vote moved a $2trn market

The politics. Electricity bills and local opposition made data centres a campaign issue. A Fox News poll in July found 70% of voters opposed a data centre near them. A CNBC poll found 56% of voters called AI and data centres a crucial or major factor in their vote; AP's poll ranked AI well below corruption and the cost of living.

Who holds the levers. Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez want a national moratorium, but any such law would face Trump's veto. Governors hold the practical tools: permits, power connections and water rights, Fortune reported.

The scale. Yahoo Finance counted about $5trn lost from 47 AI and chip stocks since 6 October. Over the same period 625 other large US stocks gained about $1.3trn. Marvell fell 28% over the week, after agreeing on Monday to buy Lumentum in an all-share deal.

The defence. Lisa Su, AMD's boss, called the market "short-sighted" about AI, 24/7 Wall St reported. TheStreet argued that slower approvals would slow new building from 2027, but that projects with money already committed carry on.

BHow it worksslide the delay

A chipmaker's share price is mostly a bet on profits years away. If a moratorium delays building, those profits still come, but later. Investors who want a return each year pay less today for money that arrives later. This is called discounting. Slide the delay and compare with Thursday's falls.

Oil's biggest weekly fall since 2020, on a bet about peace

Energy · sheet 3 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Sunday Abbas Araghchi, Iran's foreign minister, said Iran had cut its plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz to two days, at Qatar's request.
  2. Under the plan America lifts sanctions on day one and Iran reopens the strait on day two. America has not accepted.
  3. On Monday night American forces struck Iran's Qeshm Island, calling the strikes "defensive". Trump called them "minor strikes".
  4. On Wednesday Brent fell about 8.7% to $94.62. It was the first close below $100 since early October.
  5. Iran's Revolutionary Guards claimed tankers hit mines on Sunday, Wednesday and Friday. America's Central Command disputes this and says the main lanes are clear.
  6. Brent ended Friday at about $98. That is a weekly fall of 13% to 16%, depending on the contract, the biggest since 2020.
Go deeper: the talks, the strikes and the price

Why Wednesday. Traders bet that a Democratic Congress would speed the end of the war, NBC and Rigzone reported. Chuck Schumer has pledged to end it, and Vance blamed the war for the Republicans' defeat (sheet 1).

The talks. Qatar and Pakistan are mediating. Iran's demands reportedly include lifting the naval blockade, releasing frozen assets and ending the war "on all fronts". Araghchi said he spoke to 12 foreign ministers in 48 hours. CNBC reported on Friday that Iran sent a nuclear proposal through Qatar.

The fighting. The Qeshm strikes were America's first there since June. US officials tied them to Iranian fire at an American aircraft. Two projectiles hit Qeshm again on Friday night, Al Jazeera reported. None of the tanker claims has been independently confirmed.

The traffic. Data cited by Straits.live show October crossings were the highest since July, led by bulk carriers and gas tankers. The biggest crude tankers stayed away. CBS notes oil is still 60% to 70% above its pre-war level; the war began on 28 February.

BHow it worksmove the odds

A futures price is not a forecast of one outcome. It blends the outcomes traders think possible, weighted by how likely they seem. If a deal would take Brent to one price and failure to another, today's price tells you the odds the market is betting on. Set the two outcomes, then move the odds.

CWhy it matters

Oil is the main channel from the war to everyone else. About a fifth of the world's oil normally passes through Hormuz. Higher oil feeds straight into prices, which is why central banks are raising rates (sheet 5). This week showed that the odds of peace now depend on Washington's politics as much as on the Gulf. A Congress that wants out of the war moves the price before any ship moves.

DWhat to watch
  • America's answer to Qatar's two-day plan. Accepting day-one sanctions relief would signal a deal is near; rejecting it would push the odds, and the price, back up.
  • The tanker claims. Independent confirmation of a mine strike in the main lanes would raise shipping insurance and the price; continued denial by Central Command would keep traffic recovering.
  • War-powers votes in Congress once the new members sit in January.

Israel: Eisenkot gets the first try, and the same old sum

Israel · sheet 4 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Wednesday the elections committee confirmed the 27 October results. Netanyahu's camp had called the vote "stolen".
  2. On Thursday President Isaac Herzog asked each party whom it backed for prime minister. Parties with 67 of the Knesset's 120 seats named Gadi Eisenkot.
  3. Parties with 46 seats named Benjamin Netanyahu. United Torah Judaism, an ultra-Orthodox party in his bloc, named nobody.
  4. Eisenkot's 67 include Ra'am and the Joint List, two Arab-led parties. He has ruled out governing with them.
  5. On Saturday Herzog said Eisenkot would get the mandate, handed over on Sunday. Herzog warned that it was uncertain anyone could form a government.
  6. Netanyahu called for a "national unity government". Eisenkot's party rejected the idea.
Go deeper: who backed whom, and the rules

The lists. Netanyahu was backed by Likud, Religious Zionism, Otzma Yehudit, Shas and Zehut. Eisenkot was backed by his Yashar party, Together, The Democrats, Yisrael Beytenu, Ra'am and the Joint List. The Joint List's late support took him to 67, JFeed reported.

The rules. Herzog does not have to pick the candidate with most recommendations. The nominee has 28 days to form a government, with a possible 14-day extension. If that fails, the president can hand the task to someone else; if that fails too, Israel faces another election.

The defection. Al-Monitor reported that United Torah Judaism's refusal to back Netanyahu was a blow to him. It does not make the party an Eisenkot ally: ultra-Orthodox parties have usually sat with Netanyahu.

CWhy it matters

Netanyahu is not the one asked first this time. But the sum that blocked everyone last week has not changed. Eisenkot can reach 61 only by breaking a taboo: either relying on Arab-led parties, or bringing in a party that sat with Netanyahu. Each choice costs him some of his own voters. That is why both sides now talk of unity or emergency governments, and why a second election is possible.

DWhat to watch
  • United Torah Judaism. If it bargains with Eisenkot over the draft of ultra-Orthodox men into the army, a coalition becomes possible; if it returns to Netanyahu, Eisenkot is stuck.
  • Outside support from Ra'am. A pledge to back Eisenkot's government in key votes without joining it would close most of the gap; it would also hand Netanyahu a campaign line.
  • Day 28, in early December. An extension request will show how far apart the sides still are.

Oil fell, rates rose: the Bank of England turns

Money · sheet 5 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Thursday the Bank of England raised Bank Rate from 3.75% to 4%. It is the first rise since 2023.
  2. The vote was 8–1. The one dissenter, Catherine Mann, wanted a bigger rise, to 4.25%. Nobody voted to hold.
  3. The Bank now expects inflation to reach about 5% by the middle of next year. It was 3.5% in September; the target is 2%.
  4. Andrew Bailey, the governor, said the rise "should not be seen as the first in an inevitable long sequence of hikes".
  5. On Tuesday Australia's central bank raised its rate to 4.85%, its highest since 2008, on a 6–3 vote.
  6. On Friday America reported 165,000 new jobs in October, against about 59,000 expected. Unemployment fell to 4.1%.
Go deeper: the votes, the markets and the American picture

Why now. The Bank blamed the Middle East war, now in its ninth month, for higher energy prices. Bailey said the path depends on natural gas as well as oil, and gas prices "have risen sharply recently", CNBC reported. Mann warned of "non-linear" inflation risks: a sudden jump that dents trust in the Bank.

The markets. The pound barely moved, at about $1.347. The 10-year gilt yield rose about 2 basis points to 5.15%. Markets put about a 50% chance on another rise in December, CNBC reported. Bailey argued that part of the rise in market rates reflects higher risk premiums, not just expected hikes.

America. The Federal Reserve held its rate last week. After Friday's jobs report, futures put the odds of a rise at its 8–9 December meeting at about 73% to 75%. Indeed's Hiring Lab notes that health care supplied about 60% of job growth over the past year, so hiring is narrow.

Europe. The European Central Bank held its rate at 2.5% on 29 October, though it debated a rise. Christine Lagarde will leave early, on 7 February 2027.

BHow it works

Raising rates does nothing to the price of a barrel. Central banks raise them to stop an energy shock spreading. Higher bills push up the prices of other goods. Workers then ask for higher pay to keep up. Firms raise prices again to cover wages. These are called second-round effects.

Rates take a year or more to work. So the Bank acts on its forecast, not on this week's price. A 16% fall in oil (sheet 3) still leaves it 60% to 70% above where it was before the war, and gas has risen.

  • The Bank's next decision on 17 December.

California: a landslide, a new LA mayor, and a tax that met its rival

California · sheet 6 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Tuesday Democrat Xavier Becerra beat Republican Steve Hilton for governor, by about 64% to 36% in partial counts. Hilton conceded on Wednesday.
  2. On Thursday AP called the Los Angeles mayor's race for Nithya Raman, a city councillor backed by the Democratic Socialists of America. She led Karen Bass, the mayor, by about 52% to 48%.
  3. Proposition 40, a tax on billionaires' wealth, was failing by about 55% to 45%.
  4. Proposition 42, a rival measure backed by wealthy opponents of the tax, was passing with about 55%.
  5. Voters passed Proposition 1, an $11.25bn housing bond, and rejected a voter-ID measure by 58.5% to 41.4%.
  6. Postal ballots that arrive by Tuesday 10 November still count, so final margins will shift.
Go deeper: the people, the quotes and the fine print

Becerra. He was attorney-general of California and health secretary under President Biden. NBC Los Angeles says he will be the state's first Latino governor in modern times. In Sacramento he said: "If anyone wants to come after California or any one Californian, they better be ready for a fight." Hilton replied: "We're going to have a Republican governor in 2030."

Raman. Bass conceded on Thursday. Raman won although the county Democratic Party, the Chamber of Commerce and the main labour federation all backed Bass. She takes office on 14 December, 22 months after the January 2025 fires, with the rebuilding and the 2028 Olympics ahead. LAist called it a "new era of progressive politics".

The two measures. At an early count KQED had Proposition 42 on 54.6% yes, about 3.78m votes to 3.14m. GV Wire wrote that billionaires "won new protections for their wealth". Backers of the billionaire tax say they will keep pushing.

AI rules. Becerra inherits SB 53, California's law on frontier AI models. Since January it has required the largest developers to publish safety frameworks and report serious incidents. A ballot fight over children's chatbots was put off; it did not appear on Tuesday's ballot.

BHow it works

Why would opponents of a tax put their own measure on the same ballot? Because of a rule in California's constitution. When two measures passed at the same election conflict, the one with more yes votes wins.

So Proposition 42 was insurance. If voters had passed the billionaire tax narrowly, a bigger yes vote for 42 could still have cancelled it. A rival measure also splits the message: a voter unsure about taxing wealth can vote no on one and yes on the other.

The tax failed outright, so the insurance was not needed this time. But 42's protections stay in place and raise the bar for any future wealth tax.

CWhy it matters

California is home to most American AI labs and many space firms. For founders, the result removes the threat of a tax on paper wealth, the kind that falls on founders whose shares are worth a lot but who have little cash. Becerra's tone promises more fights with the Trump administration over immigration, AI rules and federal money. In Los Angeles, a mayor from the left wing of her party will run housing permits and the fire rebuild.

DWhat to watch
  • Final counts after 10 November. A narrow final margin on Proposition 42 would invite court fights over what it protects; a wide one settles the matter for years.
  • Raman's first appointments, for the fire rebuild and the police. Picks from outside City Hall would signal real change; insiders would signal continuity.
  • Whether Becerra defends SB 53 if Washington tries to override state AI laws.

The race to get pictures down faster

Space · sheet 7 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Monday Northwood Space, a ground-station start-up from El Segundo, raised $375m at a $2bn valuation. Andreessen Horowitz and DFJ Growth led; In-Q-Tel joined.
  2. On Tuesday Vantor, the former Maxar Intelligence, said it delivered satellite images to Ukrainian front-line units seven minutes after they were requested. It is the company's own claim.
  3. On Wednesday Planet released the first image from Pelican-12, its first satellite built to see detail down to about 30cm. It carries an NVIDIA computer and tests sending images down through another company's satellites.
  4. Also on Wednesday, the European Space Agency gave OHB a €554m contract for the next Sentinel-3 radar satellite, part of Europe's free Copernicus system.
  5. On Thursday BlackSky reported quarterly revenue of $42.8m, up 118% on a year earlier. It raised its forecast for the year for the second time.
Go deeper: the deals in detail

Northwood. It builds flat "Portal" antennas and "Prism" dishes, and rents time on its network. The US Space Force is its biggest named customer. Its last round, in January, raised $100m, so this one is 3.75 times bigger.

BlackSky. Adjusted EBITDA was a record $12.8m, about 30% of revenue. Net profit of $18.5m came mostly from accounting gains on warrants, not from operations. The new forecast for 2026 is $140m–150m of revenue, up from $130m–150m.

Planet. Pelican-12 launched on 20 October and is Planet's 25th satellite this year. Its relay test sends images through SES's O3b mPOWER satellites, in a higher orbit, instead of waiting to pass over a ground station. Planet's shares fell about 7% on the day, German trading press reported.

Sentinel-3. The new satellites will measure the height of the sea and land from about 2030 to 2050. The contract includes an option for a second satellite.

BHow it worksadd stations

A satellite in low orbit can only send data down while it passes over a ground station. Between passes, pictures wait on board. More stations mean shorter gaps. A relay through a satellite in a higher orbit, which stays in view far longer, can cut the wait almost to nothing. Play with both.

Greenland says yes to more American bases

Arctic · sheet 8 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Tuesday Greenland's parliament approved a security deal with America and Denmark. The vote was 16 for, none against, and 7 abstentions.
  2. Trump, Denmark's prime minister Mette Frederiksen and Greenland's premier Jens-Frederik Nielsen signed the deal in September at the UN.
  3. It lets America open two new military sites, at Narsarsuaq in the south and Mestersvig in the east. It also expands Pituffik, the base America already runs.
  4. It opens the way for Trump's "Golden Dome" missile defence. Denmark keeps sovereignty over the island.
  5. Naleraq, the party that wants fastest independence, questioned what Greenland gets. A local mayor said: "there are no benefits, no money to our society."
Go deeper: eight decades of Americans in Greenland

The old treaty. America's presence rests on the 1951 Defence of Greenland Agreement, signed two years after Denmark joined NATO, Arctic Today records. A 2004 agreement at Igaliku made Greenland itself a party. Pituffik, run by the Space Force, is America's only remaining base on the island.

The pressure. In January America and Denmark agreed to reopen the 1951 deal, after months of Trump demanding that America acquire the island, Newsweek and RFI reported. The September deal was the result. Trump claimed it gives America permanent control of Greenland's security; analysts say its legal design is unclear.

What it adds. The FDD, a Washington think-tank, argues the deal "adds sites, not strategy", because the 1951 treaty already gave America broad rights. Nielsen said Greenlanders had stayed united to show the world that the island decides its own fate, AP reported.

BWhy it matters

The Arctic is where Russian missiles would fly to reach America, and where melting ice opens new sea lanes. Bases there carry radars, which is why Golden Dome needs Greenland. For Greenland the deal swaps American pressure for a written role: it signs as a party, not as a territory being bought. The open question is money. The deal promises security, not investment, and that is why seven members abstained.

The Arctic also needs watching from space. Ships, ice and new sites in the far north are hard to see from the ground, so demand for satellite monitoring there should grow.

Science notebook: when the number falls but the risk doesn't

Science · sheet 9 of 10
ATwo heart drugs hit their targets, not their goal

This week. On Saturday two large trials at the American Heart Association's meeting reported no benefit, though both drugs did what they were designed to do to the blood.

Lp(a)HORIZON. Novartis's pelacarsen cut Lp(a) by about 78%. Yet heart attacks, strokes and heart deaths hit 12.3% of patients on the drug and 12.1% on placebo, over a median 4.7 years.

ZEUS. Novo Nordisk's ziltivekimab blocks IL-6, a signal that drives inflammation. It cut IL-6 by 76% and the inflammation marker CRP by 64%. Events were 561 on the drug and 565 on placebo, among 6,376 patients.

Go deeper: the evidence and the possible reasons

The numbers. Lp(a)HORIZON's hazard ratio was 1.02, with a 95% range of 0.90 to 1.15. ZEUS's was 0.99, range 0.88 to 1.11. Both were published in the New England Journal of Medicine on Saturday. About 21% of patients on pelacarsen stopped taking it; ZEUS saw more serious infections on the drug.

Why it might have failed. Genetic studies suggest that people born with low Lp(a) have fewer heart attacks over a lifetime. One hypothesis, reported by STAT, is that cutting it late in life cannot undo decades of damage. Steven Nissen noted that the trial included only patients who had already had an event, so it says little about prevention.

What comes next. Other Lp(a) drugs, such as olpasiran, lepodisiran and muvalaplin, are still in outcome trials. Researchers told the AHA that inflammation remains a valid target, and that dose, timing or the choice of target may explain ZEUS.

The lesson. A surrogate endpoint is a stand-in. A drug can move the stand-in without moving the outcome.

Go deeper: how genes can hint at words

The method. The Germanic study sequenced 712 new genomes and added 4,009 published ones. The Celtic study used 4,587. Researchers track ancestry that appears in a region at a certain time, then match it with archaeology and the history of the languages.

The wonder: is the wave function real?

Wonder · sheet 10 of 10
AWhat happened

This week. Quanta told the story of a theorem from 2012 that settles part of a 90-year-old argument about what quantum physics describes. It is known as PBR, after Matthew Pusey, Jonathan Barrett and Terry Rudolph.

The question. Quantum physics describes a particle with a wave function. Is that a real thing in the world? Or is it just our knowledge about some deeper reality, the way a weather forecast describes our knowledge, not the weather?

The answer. PBR proves that if there is a deeper reality, and separate systems can be prepared independently, then the wave function must be part of it. It cannot be mere knowledge.

Go deeper: ninety years of argument

1935. Einstein, with Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, argued that quantum physics is incomplete: particles have real properties that the wave function leaves out. On this view the wave function would be like a probability: a summary of what we know.

1964. John Bell set out to back Einstein's kind of realism. Instead his theorem showed that any deeper theory matching quantum predictions must link distant particles instantly. Experiments sided with quantum physics, and three of the experimenters shared the 2022 Nobel prize in physics. Quanta writes that Bell's thought experiment "revealed a weakness in Einstein's argument".

2012. PBR closed a remaining middle path: a real world underneath, with the wave function as mere knowledge of it. Quanta's summary is that physicists must either "fully embrace the notion of an objective but entirely surreal reality, or fully reject objective reality altogether."

The experiment. A team tested the theorem on IBM's Heron2 quantum processor, extending the original two-system game to five qubits. The results matched quantum predictions, ruling out knowledge-only models for small systems. Pass rates fell as qubits sat farther apart, and at larger scales noise blurred the test. The researchers had first used PBR to check that the quantum computer worked properly.

Read more: Quanta: a strange quantum theorem has killed Einstein's reality ↗ · arXiv: the PBR test on a superconducting processor ↗ · RealClearScience: the Quanta piece ↗

BHow it workspick hidden states

Take two ways to prepare a qubit, called |0⟩ and |+⟩. No measurement can tell them apart every time. If the wave function were only knowledge, both preparations could sometimes leave the qubit in the same hidden state: an overlap.

Now prepare two qubits separately. A clever joint measurement has four outcomes. Each outcome never happens for one of the four combined preparations. Pick the hidden states and see what happens.

CWhat is still open

The assumption. PBR needs preparation independence. Models that drop it can keep the wave function as knowledge; such models were built in 2012, Matt Leifer's review notes. Most physicists find giving up independence too high a price, but it is a price, not a proof.

The escape routes. PBR says nothing against views that deny any deeper, observer-free reality, such as QBism or relational quantum mechanics. One paper calls their relation to PBR "a peaceful coexistence". So Quanta's headline is strong: what died is the middle option, a real world plus a wave function that only records what we know.

Why it is a wonder. A question that sounds like philosophy became a statement that a machine can test. And the machine now uses the theorem to check that it is behaving quantum-mechanically.
