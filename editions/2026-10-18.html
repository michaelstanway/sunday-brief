Hormuz: two ships sink in a record week of attacks

The world · sheet 1 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Iran's forces stepped up attacks on shipping near the Strait of Hormuz. CBS News counted ten attacks by Tuesday.
  2. On Tuesday the Singapore-flagged container ship GFS Pride was hit, caught fire and sank. All 21 crew got off safely.
  3. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said it hit a mine. Maritime security sources told Reuters a projectile hit it.
  4. Reuters said two ships sank near Hormuz this week. They are the second and third ships sunk since the war began on 28 February.
  5. On Friday Brent crude touched $110 a barrel, its highest since May.
  6. On Saturday both America and Iran claimed control of the strait.
Go deeper: a war at sea that the midterms have paused, not stopped

The war. America and Israel have been at war with Iran since 28 February. On Friday the war reached its 231st day. Last week Donald Trump ruled out heavy strikes before the 3 November midterms. This week CBS reported him saying Iran is "ready to fold up".

The attacks. On Monday Al Jazeera reported that the Revolutionary Guards are targeting ships they say run a "US-UAE" crude shuttle out of the strait. CBS counted six attacks on Sunday and four on Tuesday. On Wednesday a second Singapore-registered ship, GFS Giselle, was struck but reached a safe anchorage under its own power.

The sinking. GFS Pride could carry 2,824 containers of the standard 20-foot size. Singapore's port authority said all 21 crew were evacuated. Iran says the ship strayed onto an "unauthorised route" and hit a mine. A security firm told Reuters there was no independent evidence for that claim.

America's view. Capt. Tim Hawkins speaks for Central Command. He told Fox News that "about 97% of the ships that have sought to transit the Strait of Hormuz have done so successfully without being hit by an Iranian projectile." Insurers and shipowners look at the other 3%.

Saudi Arabia. Houthi missiles from Yemen targeted Riyadh for a third day running on Monday, after last Saturday's airport attack killed 12. On Tuesday the Saudi-led coalition struck oil sites in western Yemen. On Friday the Houthis claimed a missile strike on Aden's airport.

Hot inflation, an AI-debt scare, and bonds rally anyway

Markets and money · sheet 2 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Wednesday America's consumer price index showed prices up 3.7% on a year earlier. In August the figure was 3.4%.
  2. Petrol rose 9.1% in the month and made up about half the rise. Core inflation was 2.6%.
  3. America's 10-year Treasury yield jumped to about 5.33% that day, then turned down.
  4. The same day shares in CoreWeave, which rents out AI chips, fell about 16%. Its bonds now yield almost 15%.
  5. On Thursday two Fed officials said there was no rush to raise rates again. The 10-year fell to about 5.08%.
  6. By Friday the 10-year had fallen about 15 basis points on the week, to 5.09%. The S&P 500 had its worst week since June.
Go deeper: why bonds rose in a week of bad inflation news

The puzzle. Hot inflation usually pushes yields up, because lenders want more to cover rising prices. This week yields fell. Prashant Newnaha of TD Securities called the rally after a hot CPI "highly counter-intuitive".

Reason one: a run to safety. Investors sold AI shares and bought Treasuries, which they see as the safest asset. Buying pushes bond prices up and yields down. CNBC reported that 20 of the most valuable AI and chip stocks lost more than $1tn in value this week. The Nasdaq fell 4.5% on the week.

Reason two: the Fed. On Thursday Fed governor Michelle Bowman said there was time to wait. Mary Daly of the San Francisco Fed said another rise is likely needed, but with no urgency. By Friday, CNBC reported, futures prices gave more than 80% odds of a hold at the 28 October meeting. Core inflation of 2.6% also gave the Fed room.

CoreWeave. CoreWeave borrows heavily to build data centres. Reuters put its debt at about $50bn. The cost of insuring its debt against default rose to about 1,000 basis points. Its chief executive, Mike Intrator, called the share fall irrational.

Britain. Andrew Bailey, the Bank of England's governor, said on Friday that second-round effects of high energy prices look "subdued" so far. In a Reuters poll, 54 of 63 economists expect Bank Rate to rise to 4.00% on 5 November. Britain's economy grew 0.1% in August. On Friday bank shares fell on fears of a bank tax in the 28 October Budget.

Ukraine: an "energy ceasefire" that never held

The world · sheet 3 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Sunday Donald Trump posted that Russia and Ukraine had agreed an "ENERGY CEASEFIRE", effective immediately.
  2. Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the news came as a surprise. On Monday he accused Russia of breaking it.
  3. On Tuesday a Russian missile hit a Kyiv power and heating plant. At least two people died, France 24 reported.
  4. On Thursday Ukraine resumed strikes on Russian oil refineries. Trump called the strikes "a disgrace".
  5. On Friday Russia struck nine regions, killed at least eight people and cut power to more than a million.
  6. The same day Trump suspended new sanctions on Russia before they took effect. Zelenskyy said the decision "seems wrong".
Go deeper: a truce announced before it was agreed

The truce. Trump announced the deal in a social-media post with no details. Zelenskyy told Axios it was "news to me". A senior Ukrainian official told Reuters Kyiv would accept a truce if Moscow did. Trump had announced a similar energy truce on 14 September.

The strikes. Tuesday's missile hit Kyiv's CHP-4 plant. Zelenskyy said Ukraine got "no early warning from our partners". On Friday, Al Jazeera reported, about 370,000 households lost power in the Volyn region alone. On Wednesday Ukraine also accused SpaceX of slowing Starlink for its drones (see sheet 6).

Trump and Zelenskyy. Trump said: "I find Zelenskyy to be more difficult to deal with than Putin." Reuters reported that Zelenskyy told the Financial Times: "We're done." He said America has proposed three-way talks with Russia by the end of October. Russia has not confirmed.

The sanctions. A new US law lets the president put tariffs of up to 100% on the five biggest buyers of Russian oil or gas. Friday's order suspends those tariffs and penalties. Lawmakers from both parties criticised it. Zelenskyy said it "definitely helps only the Russians".

The diesel deal. On 9 October Trump agreed with Vladimir Putin to let Russian diesel reach world markets, with a US licence that runs to 7 April 2027. He wants lower fuel prices before the midterms. Experts told CNBC on Saturday the deal earns Moscow little cash but may invite demands for more.

AI's money question: Anthropic opens its books

The Economist's picks · sheet 4 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Wednesday Anthropic held a private day for would-be investors at its San Francisco headquarters.
  2. It said its revenue for July to September was $16.8bn. That was about 45% more than in the quarter before.
  3. It said it was profitable on an adjusted basis for a second quarter running.
  4. It is preparing a stock-market listing. Reports put the target above $2tn. Some investors reportedly pushed for about $1.5tn.
  5. On Monday Kristalina Georgieva, head of the IMF, called AI "a slow-moving tsunami" for jobs.
  6. The Economist argues that new AI labs are raising huge sums, but most have little to show for it.
Go deeper: the cost of building AI, and who pays for it

The numbers. Reuters, citing the FT, put the previous quarter's revenue near $11.6bn. Anthropic's last private valuation was $965bn, in May. Reports say the listing could come in November, after the US midterms. Anthropic has published no full prospectus.

The pushback. On Thursday New York City's comptroller, Mark Levine, urged Anthropic to pause its listing over safety and governance concerns. Anthropic said it is talking to him. Bloomberg's Matt Levine, a different person, titled Thursday's column "Private Markets Are the New AI Markets".

The jobs warning. Georgieva spoke at the IMF and World Bank meetings in Bangkok. She said: "It is a slow-moving tsunami, but [a] tsunami nonetheless." The IMF expects about 40% of jobs worldwide to be affected by AI. The share is about 60% in advanced economies and 23% in low-income ones. Entry-level graduate jobs are hit first.

The market mood. This was a bad week for AI shares. CNBC reported that 20 of the most valuable AI and chip stocks lost more than $1tn. Debt fears at CoreWeave drove much of it (see sheet 2). TSMC, which makes most of the world's advanced chips, still reported record quarterly revenue of $46.7bn on Thursday.

The rivals. OpenAI is seeking at least $30bn at a valuation of about $1.4tn, reported in late September. Its boss says it will not list in 2026.

Brazil: a week before the run-off, Flávio stays just ahead

The world · sheet 5 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Brazil chooses its president in a run-off next Sunday, 25 October.
  2. On Thursday a Quaest poll put Flávio Bolsonaro on 46% and Lula on 44%.

Go deeper: why a narrowing lead matters

The first round. On 4 October Flávio won 47.03% and Lula 45.16%. Nobody passed 50%, so the top two meet again. Flávio is a senator and the son of Jair Bolsonaro, the jailed former president. Lula, 80, has been president since 2023 and also from 2003 to 2011.

The trend. Datafolha's 8 October poll of 2,520 people had Flávio ahead by four points, with a margin of ±2. This week's two-point gap is inside that margin. The Rio Times says Flávio leads 11 of 12 polls since 5 October.

Valid votes. Brazil counts only valid votes. On that basis the Rio Times polling average is about 52% to 48% for Flávio.

Starlink's big bet, and a throttle in Ukraine

The Economist's picks · sheet 6 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. The Economist called the expansion of SpaceX's Starlink network "a bold gamble on the future".
  2. Starlink is moving beyond home internet into mobile phones. Last week America's regulator approved up to 15,000 direct-to-device satellites.
  3. On Wednesday Ukraine said SpaceX had slowed Starlink for terminals moving faster than about 100 mph. Its drones use them for guidance.
  4. Elon Musk said any limits are set by the US military and "ultimately POTUS", Yahoo Finance reported.
  5. On Tuesday Bloomberg reported that the FAA will do a full environmental review of SpaceX's plan for 456 Starship launches a year from Texas.
Go deeper: why a network this big is a bet

The Economist's case. Its headline calls the expansion a bold gamble. A free summary of the piece was posted by the Benton Institute on Thursday. SpaceX is buying radio spectrum to build what it calls "the world's most advanced mobile network". That puts it up against America's big phone companies.

The throttle. Serhiy Beskrestnov, a Ukrainian government adviser, said the defence forces got no warning and several missions were disrupted. He linked it to friction between Zelenskyy and Trump. Poland pays for many of Ukraine's Starlink subscriptions; its foreign minister, Radosław Sikorski, publicly challenged Musk. In 2022 Musk had Starlink coverage cut in parts of Ukraine during a counter-offensive.

Launch. On Monday a Falcon 9 carried 27 Starlink satellites, the first such mission in more than three weeks, Space.com reported. Planning documents list 19 and 30 October as the earliest dates for the next two Starship flights. SpaceX has not confirmed them.

The shares. SpaceX listed in June at $135 a share. The Motley Fool said on Friday it trades about 15% above that. Up to 328.4m shares held by insiders can be sold from Saturday 24 October.

Three ghostly galaxies with almost no dark matter

Science · Quanta · sheet 8 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Wednesday Quanta reported on a third galaxy that seems to have almost no dark matter.
  2. It is called DF9. It sits on a line of galaxies near the galaxy NGC 1052, with two others like it, DF2 and DF4.
  3. Astronomers weigh a galaxy by how fast its stars move. More mass pulls harder, so the stars move faster.
  4. DF9's stars move at about 6.5 km/s. Its stars alone would give about 8.3 km/s. A normal dark-matter cloud would give about 24 km/s.
  5. The leading idea is a crash between two small galaxies. Their dark matter flew on, and their gas stayed behind and made new galaxies.
  6. If dark matter can be pulled apart from gas, it behaves like a real substance. That counts against MOND.
Go deeper: weighing a galaxy you can barely see

The method. The team, led by Michael Keim of Yale, measured the velocity dispersion of DF9's stars with the Keck telescopes in Hawaii. The result was 6.5 km/s, with an error of +3.9 and −4.3. The paper appeared in the Astrophysical Journal.

The comparison. DF9's stars weigh about 140 million Suns. That mass alone predicts 8.3 km/s. A normal dark-matter cloud predicts 24 ± 3 km/s, which the authors call a cautious low estimate. Quanta puts it simply: a normal galaxy this size would be about 100 times heavier.

The crash. DF2 was found in 2018, DF4 in 2019. In 2022 the team argued in Nature that both came from a high-speed crash of two gas-rich dwarf galaxies. In 2025 they showed other galaxies on the same line move together. It is a small version of the Bullet Cluster, where hot gas and dark matter were seen separated.

The sceptic. Stacy McGaugh of Case Western Reserve University, a supporter of MOND, calls the crash "a plausible origin story, albeit an exceptional one". He adds: "I'm not convinced that that's the only way that can happen."

Caveats. DF9's error bars are wide, so one review calls the result a consistency check, not a firm zero. The distance to the group has been disputed before. Fainter galaxies further along the line are harder to measure.

Science notebook: proofs that don't match, and a leap hour

Science · Nature and others · sheet 9 of 10
AAI maths: the proof and its code disagreemachine learning
  1. A new paper checks one of OpenAI's AI-made proofs, on the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid flow.
  2. The authors found that its Lean code does not match the written proof.
  3. In one lemma the code proves a bound with m+5 derivatives; the text claims m+4.
  4. A Lean check certifies the formal statement, not the paper a human reads.
Go deeper: what a machine check does and does not prove

The paper. Bastounis, Circelli and Hansen titled it "Navier-Stokes lost in translation" (arXiv:2610.08144). A second lemma uses a different pressure bound from the text. Anders Hansen said: "We are not saying that the natural-language proof is wrong."

Unique Games. By Saturday no independent expert had confirmed OpenAI's claimed proof of the Unique Games Conjecture, and no error had been reported. Its Lean version exists, but whether the formal statement matches Khot's conjecture is still unchecked.

The contrast. DeepMind's AlphaProof Nexus was peer-reviewed in Science on 8 October. It proved 9 of 353 open Erdős problems written in Lean, about 2.5%. Gemini writes proofs, Lean checks them, and errors go back to Gemini.

Benchmarks. On Friday Quanta interviewed Moritz Hardt about his new book. He calls AI benchmarks "flawed but fixable" and describes a "benchmarking crisis". OpenAI also gave $5m to a Simons Foundation effort to help mathematicians check AI results.

BCompression: 3D scenes squeezed by image codecscompression
  1. A new paper, RDCompGS, compresses 3D Gaussian splats.
  2. It maps each blob's settings onto 2D images, then uses standard image codecs on them.
  3. It adds rate–distortion optimisation, because scenes that look equally good can cost very different numbers of bits.
Go deeper: why reuse image codecs

The idea. Image codecs are fast, tuned and built into hardware. If 3D data can be laid out as images, decades of codec work apply at once. The paper's premise is that the layout and the bit budget, not the codec, decide most of the cost.

Standards. On 7 October the JPEG committee said JPEG XE, a standard for event-camera data, had become an International Standard. Event cameras record only pixels that change, which cuts data volumes for fast scenes.

Caveat. The RDCompGS paper is new and not yet peer-reviewed. No independent tests were found.

CThe leap second is retiredphysics
  1. On Thursday the world's timekeepers voted to stop adding leap seconds.
  2. UTC may now drift up to an hour from the Earth's rotation.
  3. The change starts on 20 May 2027, well before the 2035 deadline set in 2022.
  4. The Earth has been spinning faster, so a first-ever negative leap second was becoming likely.
Go deeper: why a missing second scared engineers

The risk. Software is used to clocks gaining a second, not losing one. A negative leap second has never happened, so much code was never tested for it. One timekeeper put the chance of needing one by 2035 at about 30%.

The vote. The General Conference on Weights and Measures voted by majority, not unanimously. Nature reports that an hour's drift may not need correcting for more than 1,000 years.

Off the map: Britain's northern spaceport, and the oldest shipwreck

History and places · sheet 10 of 10
AUnst, Shetlanda place
  1. Unst is the most northerly inhabited island in Britain. It is home to SaxaVord, the UK's only licensed vertical-launch spaceport.
  2. On Thursday the government picked ClearSpace to lead a mission to remove two dead British satellites from orbit, for more than £60m.
  3. The spacecraft will launch from SaxaVord in 2029. Its robotic arms will grab each satellite and drag it down to burn up.
  4. The public is invited to name it. The Register's headline: "Get in the bin!"
Go deeper: a launch site at the edge of the map

The mission. ClearSpace, based in Luxembourg with a British arm, beat Astroscale and Starfish Space. The government calls the mission a "world-first". The first satellite should come down around 2030 and the second by 2032. Entries to name the craft close on 10 December.

The spaceport. SaxaVord is licensed for up to 30 launches a year, but nothing has launched from it yet. The German firm RFA called off a planned launch in August. On 9 October the British and German governments named it a possible hub for European access to space.

The island. Off Unst's northern tip stands Muckle Flugga, whose name comes from Old Norse for "large steep-sided island". Its lighthouse, Britain's most northerly, dates from the Crimean War. A temporary light shone in 1854, and David and Thomas Stevenson began the tower in 1855. Gannets and puffins nest at the nearby Hermaness reserve.

BThe Kekova shipwreck, Turkeyhistory
  1. Divers off Kekova Island, in Antalya province, found a ship that may be the oldest known wreck in the world.
  2. Archaeologists date it to about 4,400 to 4,700 years ago, in the Early Bronze Age.
  3. It lies about 30 metres down. It held three stone anchors and more than 30 clay jars, called amphorae.
  4. Some jars still hold a glossy residue.
Go deeper: dating a ship with no wood

The team. The dig is led by Hakan Öniz of Akdeniz University. Reports quote him calling the wreck a "time capsule at the bottom of the sea". The preliminary report is in the Journal of Maritime Archaeology.

The method. No wood survived, so radiocarbon dating was not possible. The team dated the wreck from the shape and decoration of its jars. It compared them with Early Bronze Age jars from Egypt and the Middle East. Clay tests suggest some came from workshops in the Jordan Valley.

What was inside. Karin Sowada, who examined the jars, told the BBC the residue "could be olive oil, it could be wine, it could be tree resins". The jars may also have been reused.

Caution. The university says the claim "requires further archaeological verification". A team member writing in The Conversation called the dates preliminary.
