AI claims hundreds of maths results, and a famous conjecture

Science · Quanta · sheet 1 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Tuesday OpenAI posted hundreds of maths results written by an unreleased AI model.
  2. Early reports counted 377 results. Later counts found 722 manuscripts, grouped into 372 families.
  3. The headline claim is a proof of the Unique Games Conjecture, posed in 2002.
  4. Only part of the batch has proofs checked by a computer in Lean. Counts of how much differ.
  5. Mathematicians are still reading. Some praised the work; others objected to the method.
  6. Quanta told how an MIT team rushed out its own related result before OpenAI's release.
Go deeper: what was claimed, and what is checked

The release. OpenAI said an internal model was given about 4,000 problems. A spokesperson said it produced "almost every one of the results in response to a single prompt handed to a single AI agent". The model is not public. The batch spans number theory, algebra, logic, topology and theoretical computer science, Interesting Engineering reported. The Wall Street Journal, reposted by Human Progress, said it also touched three of the five unsolved Millennium Prize Problems.

What a Lean check proves. One count found 162 of the 722 papers with a Lean-verified main result, about 22%. Another counted 235 of the 372 families with some Lean formalisation. A Lean check shows the proof follows from the statement as typed into Lean. It does not show that the statement matches the original problem, or that the result is new or important.

Praise. Rutgers mathematician Alex Kontorovich wrote on X that a human proof of the claimed "quasi-Riemann hypothesis" would be "an instant Fields Medal, no questions asked". Daniel Litt of Toronto said mathematicians have "a lot of exciting work to do".

Objections. An advisory group at the Institute for Advanced Study had asked labs to publish the model, the prompts and the computing used per problem. OpenAI released only averages. Terence Tao called the pace "insane", and argued that solving open problems at industrial scale "harvests" them and leaves fields "less fertile". In September, 25 Fields medallists signed a letter against using famous problems as AI benchmarks, TechCrunch reported.

Caveat. OpenAI's chief executive, Sam Altman, reportedly called the results claims that outside mathematicians have not yet confirmed. One report says OpenAI withdrew three papers a day later over a sign error.

Brazil: a Bolsonaro edges ahead of Lula

The world · sheet 2 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Brazil voted last Sunday. Flávio Bolsonaro came first with 47.03%. Lula took 45.16%.
  2. Polls had put Lula slightly ahead. Nobody passed 50%, so the two meet in a run-off on 25 October.
  3. The gap, about 1.9 points, is the narrowest first-round gap since direct elections returned in 1989.
  4. Flávio's Liberal Party won the most seats in both houses of Congress.
  5. On Thursday Datafolha's first run-off poll put Flávio on 49% and Lula on 45%.
  6. Trump called the result "a big victory -- close, but very, very big victory."
Go deeper: why the polls missed, and what comes next

The numbers. Flávio won about 56 million votes and Lula almost 53 million, Al Jazeera reported. Abstention was 21.08%, the highest since 1998; AP counted a record 33.5 million non-voters. The two eliminated candidates with about 2% each, Ronaldo Caiado and Renan Santos, say they will back neither man.

The polling miss. Datafolha's last pre-election poll had Lula ahead in a run-off by 48% to 45%. That lead was inside the margin of error, so the result was not a shock in statistical terms. But it reversed the order most voters expected.

In their words. Lula said: "The result is often unexpected. We can never get everything we wanted." Flávio said: "Lula has fought seven presidential elections. He's worried about power. I am worried about the future of Brazil." Both quotes are LBC's translations.

The family. Flávio's father, Jair Bolsonaro, is in prison for a coup plot. Outlook India reports that Flávio's stepmother, Michelle Bolsonaro, won a Senate seat; we found only one source for that.

What follows. On Thursday's Datafolha poll, Flávio leads 52% to 48% among valid votes. More voters reject Lula (46%) than Flávio (43%). The next president takes office on 5 January 2027.

The Gulf: no strikes on Iran before the midterms, but Riyadh is hit

The world · sheet 3 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Thursday Trump said America will not attack Iran before the 3 November midterms.
  2. He said the naval blockade stays, and cited "productive discussions" with Iran.
  3. The same day, reports that the Pentagon was preparing for possible renewed "major combat operations" had pushed Brent crude above $104.
  4. On Saturday an attack on Riyadh's King Khalid airport killed at least 12 people and wounded 309.
  5. A diplomatic source blamed a Houthi missile. It was the third attack on the airport in a week.
  6. Trump said America "may" join the fight against the Houthis.
Go deeper: the pledge, the tankers and the Houthis

The pledge. Trump posted on Truth Social: "we will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the Midterm Elections to be held in the United States on November 3rd". Late on Wednesday he had said he did not want a deal, CNBC reported. Al Jazeera asked whether the post signals a return to war after the vote. Last week he told TIME heavier strikes after the midterms were "possible".

Tankers and oil. CNBC counted nine tankers attacked in and around Hormuz in the week to Thursday. Brent rose 4% to $104.28 that day, then gave back some gains after Trump's post. Tanker flows through Hormuz were about 9.5 million barrels a day in the week to Tuesday, about 30% below pre-war levels, the tracker Kpler estimated.

Riyadh. Saudi Arabia's civil aviation authority gave the toll: four Saudis, two Bangladeshis and one person each from the US, Jordan, Palestine, Syria, Sudan and Egypt. Reuters-based reports called it the deadliest attack in the kingdom in more than a decade. No alert sounded. The airport suspended flights. The Saudi-led coalition vowed to retaliate.

Diplomacy. On Saturday the Kremlin said Vladimir Putin had passed Iran's proposal for ending the war to Trump, CNBC reported. Iran's president reportedly called the talks "meaningless".

Long yields hit 2002 highs, then buyers come back

Markets and money · sheet 4 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Wednesday America's 10-year Treasury yield touched about 5.35–5.37%. That is its highest since 2002.
  2. The same day Britain's 30-year gilt yield hit about 6.04%, its highest since 1998.
  3. Then auctions drew solid demand. The 30-year sold on Thursday at 5.618%, the highest auction yield since 2000.
  4. By Friday the 10-year had eased to about 5.24%, below last Friday's 5.28%.
  5. Wednesday's Fed minutes showed most officials saw one more rise this year as likely appropriate.
  6. Stocks shrugged it off. The S&P 500 rose about 1.2% on the week.
Go deeper: why yields rose, and why they stopped

The push up. The 10-year has risen about 60 basis points since late July, while crude oil rose about 20%. Dear oil keeps inflation up, so lenders demand more. CNBC argued on Monday that the rise does not yet signal a US "fiscal apocalypse".

The turn. The $39bn 10-year auction on Wednesday sold at 5.300%, and demand was solid, CNBC said. Thursday's $22bn 30-year sale went at 5.618%. David Zervos, an adviser to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, said yields are "really, really high" but can come down soon.

The Fed. The Fed raised its rate in September to 3.75–4.00%, on a 12–0 vote. The minutes show most officials favoured one more rise; some preferred a pause. After last week's weak jobs report, markets put the chance of a rise on 28 October at only about 17–25%.

Britain. Bank Rate is 3.75%. In September three of nine policymakers voted to raise it. MUFG and others expect a rise on 5 November. The 10-year gilt hit 5.53%, a 19-year high, after the oil jump. See sheet 8 for the Budget.

Next week. US CPI for September comes out on Wednesday 14 October. Forecasts expect headline inflation to rise from 3.4% to about 3.6–3.7%. A New York Fed study, reported by CNBC, found that inflation on many everyday goods came entirely from tariffs.

America freezes green cards at Microsoft and seven others

The world · sheet 5 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Thursday Vice-President JD Vance suspended Microsoft and Adobe from PERM.
  2. PERM is the first step of most green cards that employers sponsor.
  3. Labor Secretary Keith Sonderling named six more: Cognizant, Infosys, Tata Consultancy Services, Wipro, HCL and Capgemini.
  4. The eight cannot file new cases. Pending cases are frozen.
  5. There is no end date. Vance said it lasts "as long as it needs to".
  6. It does not touch the H-1B visa itself, Axios reported.
Go deeper: why these firms, and what it means for workers

The charge. Vance said Microsoft laid off about 6,000 US workers last year while it benefited from 6,300 H-1Bs and nearly 3,000 green cards. He called it a "scandalous system". Microsoft says it pays H-1B staff the same as others doing comparable work.

Who is hit. Six of the eight firms are big IT outsourcing companies. Al Jazeera reports the move will hit thousands of IT workers, many of them Indian. Critics quoted in its headline call such workers "indentured servants", because their path to residence depends on one employer.

The wider push. In September Trump told agencies to weigh an employer's recent layoffs of similar US workers in H-1B cases. He also renewed a $100,000 fee on some H-1B applications for another year. TIME reports the government is also examining nine universities, including Harvard, Yale and Stanford, over their use of exchange visas.

For founders. We found no change this week to the O-1 visa for people with extraordinary ability, which many founders use. The PERM freeze matters for start-ups mainly through hiring: workers stuck in a frozen green-card case may find it harder to move jobs.

Space and EO: 15,000 phone satellites, and radar for California

Space and EO · sheet 6 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Tuesday America's FCC approved up to 15,000 new SpaceX satellites that talk straight to ordinary phones.
  2. It also waived a rule that SpaceX must partner with a phone carrier. SpaceX can now sell mobile service on its own spectrum.
  3. The same day California signed up for ICEYE's SAR flood and wildfire data across all state agencies.
  4. On Friday Lithuania's Astrolight said its laser link from orbit to a ground station in Greece runs routinely at 1 Gbit/s.
  5. Fortastra, a Torrance start-up that builds manoeuvrable security spacecraft, raised $30m.
  6. On Monday a dead Soviet satellite, Meteor 2-7, broke into tens of pieces.
Go deeper: the details for EO operators

The FCC grant. The satellites would fly in nine shells between 326 km and 335 km, in very low Earth orbit. They use spectrum SpaceX agreed to buy from EchoStar. Half must be working by 7 October 2032; SpaceX plans to start launches late next year. The FCC deferred two Ka-band ranges, 20.2–21.2 GHz and 30.0–31.0 GHz, because of heavy federal use. SpaceX's current direct-to-cell fleet is about 650 satellites at about 4 Mbit/s per user. It claims up to 150 Mbit/s for the new one, TechSpot reported.

ICEYE and California. Governor Gavin Newsom announced the deal as the state reopened its emergency operations centre at Mather, near Sacramento, before the El Niño season. It covers mapping flood extent, estimating water depth and assessing damage through cloud and smoke, with 24/7 monitoring. Cal OES has used the tools since February. No price was given.

Lasers to the ground. Astrolight's ATLAS-1 terminal went from first light to routine 1 Gbit/s operation within a week, Via Satellite reported. ESA backed the work. Optical links carry more data than radio, but cloud can block them, so operators need several ground stations.

Money and defence. Fortastra's Series A was led by First In; its two Watchdog prototypes flew on last week's Transporter-18. Payload reported that Seattle's Kapta Space raised $24m for low-cost radar that tracks moving targets. A stopgap US budget averted a shutdown on 1 October, but Breaking Defense says it blocks more than 150 new military programmes. Late in the week a Falcon 9 carried 21 more Space Development Agency data-relay satellites, so 84 of 126 are now in orbit.

Debris. LeoLabs detected the Meteor 2-7 break-up from Western Australia. The satellite launched in 1981 and first broke up in 2004. LeoLabs says it is part of a run of break-ups over six months.

Go deeper: radio versus laser downlinks

The sum. 1 Gbit/s for 480 seconds is 480 gigabits, or 60 gigabytes, per pass. That is 2.5 times the 400 Mbit/s radio link in last week's figure. The model ignores link set-up time and protocol overhead.

The catch. A laser beam to the ground fails in cloud. So operators spread optical stations over dry sites and keep radio as a fallback. Astrolight's first station is in Greece.

The spectrum angle. Radio downlinks need licensed bands. This week the FCC held back two Ka-band ranges from SpaceX because of heavy federal use. Crowded bands are one reason operators are testing lasers.

Ukraine: a birthday barrage kills 22 in Pryluky

The world · sheet 7 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Overnight into Wednesday Russia fired about 70 missiles and about 130 drones at Ukraine. Wednesday was Putin's birthday.
  2. They hit energy and homes in at least 12 regions.
  3. A Kh-101 cruise missile hit a five-storey block in Pryluky, in Chernihiv region. It killed 22 people, five of them children.
  4. On Thursday Volodymyr Zelensky visited Pryluky with Britain's foreign secretary, Ed Miliband.

The aid. Last Sunday German Chancellor Friedrich Merz made an unannounced visit to Kyiv and pledged another €1bn in military aid. A Russian strike hit a Kyiv bridge during his visit, Al Jazeera reported.

The diplomacy. Fortune reports that Trump is striking a diesel deal with Putin, weeks after signing Russia sanctions. On Saturday the Kremlin said Putin had passed an Iranian peace proposal to Trump (see sheet 3).

Why it matters. Russia keeps hitting energy before winter. Air defence is the scarce resource: every interceptor fired at a drone is one fewer for a missile.

Britain: Burnham's first test, and a Budget boxed in by gilts

Britain · sheet 8 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Thursday Labour held Holborn and St Pancras, Keir Starmer's old seat, in a by-election.
  2. Labour's Sagal Abdi-Wali won 12,292 votes. Green Party leader Zack Polanski won 8,928.
  3. It was the first by-election under Prime Minister Andy Burnham. Turnout was the lowest in any English by-election this parliament.
  4. Chancellor John Healey gives his first Budget on 28 October.
  5. This week the 30-year gilt yield hit about 6.04%, its highest since 1998.
  6. Higher borrowing costs shrink the room the Budget has under the fiscal rules.
Go deeper: the by-election, the Budget and start-ups

The result. Labour won about 45% of the vote and the Greens about 33%, a majority of 3,364. Labour talked of a "Burnham bounce". Polanski stays on as Green leader.

The squeeze. In September Deutsche Bank estimated the bond sell-off could cut the Chancellor's headroom from £26bn to about £13.8bn. Bank of America expects the Budget to add £15bn of borrowing. The chief executive of the adviser deVere said Britain is being "slowly Truss'd".

Rates. Markets and banks including MUFG expect the Bank of England to raise Bank Rate from 3.75% on 5 November. Higher rates and higher gilt yields both push up fixed mortgage deals.

For start-ups. Since 6 April, the EMI share-option scheme has been open to bigger firms. Limits are now £120m of gross assets, 500 staff, £6m of company options and 15-year option terms. Speculation ahead of the Budget includes changes to capital gains tax.

London listings. Airtel Money began trading in London on Friday, valued at about $7bn. It is the biggest London IPO since Wise in 2021. It raised no new money; existing holders sold 270 million shares at £1.96.

Science notebook: a Nobel week

Science · Nature and others · sheet 9 of 10
APhysics: ghost particles in Antarctic icephysics
  1. On Tuesday Francis Halzen, 82, of the University of Wisconsin–Madison won the physics prize alone.
  2. It honours the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and its discovery of high-energy neutrinos from space.
  3. IceCube is a cubic kilometre of ice under the South Pole, fitted with light sensors.
  4. When a rare neutrino hits the ice, it makes a faint flash. The sensors record it.
Go deeper: why a kilometre of ice

The idea. Neutrinos almost never interact, so a detector must be huge to catch a few. Times Higher Education credits Halzen as the first to see that deep South Pole ice could serve as the detector. It also calls him the force behind building it. IceCube was completed in 2011.

The discovery. The Nobel citation reads: "for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin." Unlike light, neutrinos fly straight from their source through dust and gas, so they point back to violent objects far away.

The prize. Halzen, who was born in Belgium, takes the whole 12 million Swedish kronor, about $1.2m.

Read more: Nobel press release ↗ · Nature ↗ · Times Higher Education ↗

BChemistry: why life is one-handedchemistry
  1. On Wednesday Henri Kagan, 95, of France and Kenso Soai, 76, of Japan shared the chemistry prize.
  2. Many molecules come in left- and right-handed mirror forms. Life uses only one hand of each. This is homochirality.
  3. Kagan showed a catalyst with a small excess of one hand can give a much larger excess in the product.
  4. Soai found a reaction whose product speeds up its own formation. A tiny imbalance grows.
Go deeper: amplifying a small imbalance

Nonlinear effects. In the simple picture, a catalyst that is 60% one hand gives a product that is 60% one hand. Kagan found cases where the product's excess is far larger than the catalyst's.

Autocatalysis. In the Soai reaction, first reported in 1995, a zinc reagent adds to an aldehyde, and the product catalyses its own formation. The Nobel committee's background paper says the product can form with a "considerable" excess of one hand. That holds even from reagents with no handedness and no added catalyst.

In their words. Committee chair Heiner Linke said: "Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai have provided a solution to a chemical mystery that is over a century old: how homochirality can emerge spontaneously."

Off the map: an island of tortoises, and Neanderthal carpenters

History and places · sheet 10 of 10
AAldabra Atoll, Seychellesa place
  1. Aldabra is a UNESCO World Heritage atoll, far from Seychelles' main islands.
  2. A new study puts its giant tortoises at almost 183,000.
  3. The usual comparison is about 100,000 in the late 1990s. One report says the number once fell below 1,000.
  4. But the count probably did not jump. Older surveys missed tortoises hiding in scrub and shade.
Go deeper: how do you count 183,000 tortoises?

The study. The Seychelles Islands Foundation and the University of Exeter led it, in the journal Ecology and Evolution. It models five years of survey counts, from 2018 to 2022. The population looked fairly stable over that period.

The method. Many tortoises hide in dense scrub or shade, so surveys miss them. The study corrects for the animals that surveys miss, so its total is larger than older counts.

A caution. The university calls it the largest-ever estimate. But an older paper cites a 1978 estimate of 150,000. Different methods give different totals, so comparisons over decades are rough.

BNeanderthal woodworkers near Barcelonaa piece of history
  1. On Wednesday a team published 597 wooden remains and imprints from Abric Romaní, a rock shelter in Catalonia.
  2. They date from about 62,000 to 40,000 years ago, and were made by Neanderthals.
  3. The finds include poles, a tripod, possible digging sticks, two spears and a club-like object.
  4. Four charred pieces of bark may have been trays or food containers.
  5. Wood rarely survives so long. Here, rock-forming minerals hardened around it as it decayed.
  6. Earlier wooden spears came from kill sites by lakes. Abric Romaní was a home.
Go deeper: what the wood tells us

The site. Abric Romaní, in Catalonia, has been dug for more than 40 years. The study, led by Palmira Saladié of the IPHES-CERCA institute and Rovira i Virgili University, is the first full analysis of its wooden finds. It appeared in Science Advances.

How it survived. Slow floods covered the wood. Calcium carbonate from the travertine rock then hardened around each piece as it decayed. In some cases only the shape remains, as an imprint in the stone.

Why it matters. Most wood rots within years, so stone tools dominate the record of early humans. That skews our picture of Neanderthals. Science magazine called the trove the first of its kind. The poles may have held up shelters. The club-like object is unlike anything known from the period.

Caveat. Outlets differ slightly on the number of layers and objects. The uses of most objects are inferred from their shape and wear.
