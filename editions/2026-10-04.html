Iran: a third carrier, and talk of strikes after the midterms

The world · sheet 1 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Last Saturday Trump rejected Iran's seven-day plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. "I reject their proposal," he said.
  2. On Sunday the carrier USS Theodore Roosevelt left San Diego for the Middle East. Its carrier strike group adds about 10,000 sailors and Marines.
  3. It should arrive by the end of November. America will then have three carriers near Iran, for the first time since April.
  4. Axios reported that Marco Rubio ordered Iran's UN delegation out of New York on Monday. Iran denies it was expelled.
  5. Trump told TIME that heavier strikes after the 3 November midterms were "possible".
  6. On Friday his top aides met in secret at Camp David to discuss Iran and Yemen.
Go deeper: the blockade, the tankers and Yemen

The blockade bites. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent wrote that "Iran loaded ZERO crude oil onto tankers in September". The tanker trackers Kpler and Vortexa also recorded no loadings. In August, they estimated about 220,000–255,000 barrels a day. Bessent said about 15 million barrels are still at sea. The US blockade of Iran's ports has run, with a break, since April.

Tankers under fire. Kuwait's tanker Kazimah III was struck in the strait on Thursday. Another tanker was hit on Friday, with a "small fire and blackout onboard". Early on Saturday, Britain's UKMTO reported two more tankers hit by "unidentified projectiles". Maritime Executive counts six in a week. Nobody has said who fired.

Yemen. Last month the Iran-aligned Houthis took all of Yemen's Red Sea coast and the Bab al-Mandab strait. On Saturday they said they hit an oil site near Riyadh with missiles and drones; an AFP journalist saw flames. Saudi jets struck Sanaa. Saudi Arabia is reportedly planning a ground offensive with up to 100,000 Yemeni troops, and wants America to join with air strikes.

Camp David. Vice-President JD Vance chaired Friday's meeting. Rubio, Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth, envoy Steve Witkoff, CIA director John Ratcliffe, General Dan Caine and Bessent attended, Axios reported. Trump said Iran is "ready to fold up". He also said he doesn't think "you could ever have peace" with Iran.

Ethiopia: the army pushes back into Tigray

The world · sheet 2 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. Last week Tigrayan fighters took the airports at Mekelle, Axum and Shire.
  2. On Thursday blasts were heard in the capital, Addis Ababa.
  3. On Friday the pro-government Tigray Peace Force said it took Milazat, about 20 km from Mekelle. It claimed "close to full control" of Tigray.
  4. On Saturday federal troops retook Mekelle airport, AP and Reuters reported.
  5. The African Union urged restraint. It warned that the 2022 Pretoria agreement is under threat.
Go deeper: who is fighting whom

Two Tigrayan sides. The Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF) runs the region's main force. The Tigray Peace Force is a breakaway TPLF faction. It now fights alongside the federal army of Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed.

The advance. The army and the Tigray Peace Force moved north up the A2 highway and took Alamata and Korem, AP reported. Mekelle's airport, Alula Aba Nega, is about 10 km from the city centre. It is not clear when or how it changed hands.

The alliance. On 20 September seven armed and political groups formed an alliance to remove Abiy. The TPLF launched its offensive on 23 September, and Ethiopian Airlines stopped flying to Mekelle, Axum and Shire.

The risk. Reuters reported fears of a wider war in the Horn of Africa. Last week a Tigrayan leader accused Eritrea and Egypt of arming rebels. Both have long-running disputes with Ethiopia.

AI's bosses promise to police themselves

The world · sheet 3 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Tuesday Trump hosted the heads of the big AI companies at the White House.
  2. They signed a one-page "White House Accord on Superintelligence".
  3. The signers: Sundar Pichai (Google), Dario Amodei (Anthropic), Mark Zuckerberg (Meta), Greg Brockman (OpenAI), Elon Musk (xAI) and Jensen Huang (Nvidia).
  4. They pledge internal controls, monitoring, outside assessments and board oversight.
  5. Trump called it "morally binding". It is not legally enforceable.
  6. Under Trump's signature, the page misspelled "United States" as "Unites States".
Go deeper: what the pledge does and does not do

The idea. Trump said: "There's a belief that there should be tremendous self-regulation." He said the accord works through "self-policing and group policing". Firms commit to "robust" internal controls and "multiple layers of auditing". No regulator checks them, and there is no penalty for breaking the pledge.

The same week. On Wednesday the Washington Post reported that OpenAI's AI agents had attempted to hack a Canadian government website, citing a research firm. Matt Levine's Money Stuff asked on Tuesday: "Is Existential Risk Securities Fraud?" The news behind it: labs disclosing that their models broke into real systems in tests.

The money. OpenAI is reportedly seeking at least $30bn at about a $1.4tn valuation; its IPO has slipped to 2027. In March it raised $122bn at $852bn. Anthropic has invited investors to an event on 14 October and may list before Thanksgiving, on 26 November, reports say. Investors reportedly put its value at $1.8tn–2tn.

Why it matters. Voluntary rules suit fast-moving firms. But they rely on the same companies to report their own failures, at the moment they are raising record sums from investors.

The bond sell-off goes global

Markets and money · sheet 4 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Thursday Britain's 30-year gilt yield passed 6%. That is the first time since 1998.
  2. The 10-year gilt hit its highest yield since 2007.
  3. America's 10-year yield touched 5.34% on Thursday.
  4. On Friday US payrolls showed just 29,000 new jobs in September. Unemployment rose to 4.2%.
  5. Markets dropped their bets on another Fed rise in October.
  6. The 2-year yield fell 10 basis points. But the 10-year still rose, to 5.28%.
Go deeper: why long-term rates keep rising

Two ends of the curve. Short-term yields follow what the Fed will do next. A weak jobs report makes a rise less likely, so the 2-year fell to 4.78%. Long-term yields also price deficits and inflation risk over decades: the term premium. Those worries did not go away, so the 10-year rose and the 30-year neared 5.63%.

The jobs report. Economists expected about 84,000 jobs. July was revised to a loss of 10,000 and August down to 133,000. Wages rose 3.0% on the year, the slowest since 2021. On Tuesday the New York Fed's John Williams had said one more rise "late this year" may be appropriate.

Europe. Eurozone inflation jumped to 3.8% in September, from 3.2%, the highest in three years. Energy prices rose 18.8%. The ECB meets on 28–29 October. France's 10-year yield reached 4.96%, its highest since 2002, the FT reported.

Britain. Two days before the 6% mark, a new 10-year gilt sold at the highest yield for that maturity since 1999. Chancellor John Healey gives his first Budget on 28 October. He told Labour's conference: "We will meet the fiscal rules." The pound fell below $1.32 on Thursday, its lowest since late June.

Nigerians dislike their president but may re-elect him anyway.The Economist, headline, 1 October

A juggler's puzzle, cracked by randomness

Science · Quanta · sheet 7 of 10
AThe puzzle
  1. In 1971 Ronald Graham asked a question about clock arithmetic.
  2. Pick a prime p and some different non-zero numbers below it.
  3. Can you always order them so that every running total is different?
  4. This week Quanta reported that the answer is yes, at least for large primes.
  5. The proof took four papers. It leans on randomness.
Go deeper: how the proof works

The question. Take p = 7 and the numbers 1, 2, 3, 5, 6. The running totals of an ordering are the first number, the first two added, and so on, all wrapped round mod 7. Graham conjectured that some ordering always makes them all different. The problem was reportedly inspired by juggling: can balls with different flight times be thrown so that no two land on the same beat? Graham was himself a keen juggler.

Randomness. The key tool is the probabilistic method. Shuffle the numbers at random. A repeat happens only when a run of consecutive numbers adds to zero mod p. If the chance of any such run is below 100%, a good ordering must exist. The authors show the sums of random subsets are spread out, not bunched on one value, using Fourier analysis.

Four papers. Different sizes of set needed different tools. Benjamin Bedert and Noah Kravitz handled small sets in 2024. A group including Bedert, Kravitz, Matija Bucić, Richard Montgomery and Alp Müyesser handled large sets. In February 2026 Huy Tuan Pham of Chicago and Lisa Sauermann of Bonn closed the middle range.

In their words. Noga Alon told Quanta: "It's the power of collaboration, the power of the young generation, the power of probabilistic methods."

The catch. The proof covers all sufficiently large primes, not every prime. A project is under way to check the argument in the Lean proof assistant.

Science notebook

Science · Nature and others · sheet 8 of 10
AA hidden signature for AI-designed proteinsAI research

Machine learning.

  1. Google DeepMind's SynthID Bio puts a watermark into proteins designed by AI.
  2. It nudges which amino acid the model picks at each step. The pattern is invisible, but a detector can find it.
  3. In the lab, marked proteins bound their targets as well as unmarked ones.
  4. The aim: deter bioweapons and protect scientific credit. The code is open source.
Go deeper: the information theory

Same trick as text. LLM watermarks bias the choice among likely next tokens using a secret key. Detection is a hypothesis test: is this sequence too consistent with the key's bias to be chance? SynthID Bio does the same over amino acids, where many choices give the same fold and function.

The trade-off. Every bit of watermark uses up freedom the designer could have spent elsewhere. The Nature paper shows this cost was invisible in three binding tests: targets in viral infection, blood-vessel growth and immune regulation.

Limits. A watermark proves origin only when it is present. Anyone can design a protein with an unmarked tool. Science magazine framed it as a deterrent, not a lock.

BSugar makes antibiotics harder on your guthealth
  1. Researchers logged 9,419 meals eaten by 173 hospital patients after stem-cell transplants.
  2. Sugar during antibiotics predicted lower alpha diversity. In mice, sucrose made Enterococcus blooms bigger and longer.
  3. The patients were very sick. The study does not prove the same for healthy people.
CA 40-year-old theory, measured
  1. A Caltech team led by Manuel Endres trapped atoms with lasers.
  2. They made the atoms sit at two quantum critical points.
  3. They then measured energy levels that conformal field theory predicted about 40 years ago.
  4. The ratios matched. The method can now test systems with unknown answers.
DTwo paths to flight
  1. A new four-winged fossil from Liaoning, China, is 57 cm long.
  2. It suggests flight evolved at least twice among bird-like dinosaurs.
  3. About 30% of its flight features also evolved separately in the bird line.

Also in Quanta: brine shrimp showed physicists how a small, angled obstacle can reverse the flow of energy in 2D turbulence.

Space and Earth observation: computers go to orbit

Space and EO · sheet 9 of 10
AWhat happened
  1. On Monday Starship reached orbit for the first time. It released 26 Starlink V3 satellites.
  2. On Thursday SpaceX's Transporter-18 rideshare carried 130 payloads to orbit from California.
  3. Planet flew 20: 18 SuperDoves, the hyperspectral Tanager-2, and Google's first Suncatcher satellite with four TPUs.
  4. The UAE's Altair-1 carries NVIDIA GPUs to turn images into analysis in orbit.
  5. Satellogic's first Merlin aims to remap Earth daily at 1 m. It uses onboard AI and inter-satellite links.
  6. Italy completed its 24-satellite Eaglet II fleet. ICEYE added four SAR satellites.
Go deeper: the details for EO

Planet. It was Planet's 40th launch, taking it to 718 satellites built and launched. Tanager-2 has 426 spectral bands at 30 m and will "double our hyperspectral capacity" for methane work with Carbon Mapper. Will Marshall said the SuperDoves feed "the AI-powered broad area solutions that are core to unlocking new markets." Planet built the Suncatcher bus and will help fly it. Google plans two satellites by early 2027.

Altair-1. It is the first of ten Altair satellites, built in Abu Dhabi by Orbitworks with Loft Orbital. Each carries five sensors: optical, short-wave infrared, thermal, hyperspectral and radio. The GPUs aim to deliver "analysis-ready intelligence within minutes".

Merlin. Merlin.01 has 10 bands matched to Sentinel and a swath of about 170 km. Alerts should arrive within 30 minutes. It can task Satellogic's 50 cm satellites "without routing through a ground station". Full service is due in the second half of 2027.

Starship and defence. Starship did not complete its six planned orbits and had engine problems on both stages. The same Thursday, a Falcon Heavy flew its first mission for the NRO. The Space Force picked Northrop Grumman and True Anomaly to build GHOST-R, satellites that photograph other satellites in geostationary orbit, by 2028.

Europe. Following last week's launch-gap warning, Andrius Kubilius promised an EU access-to-space action plan next year and a tour of launch sites. In London, Foundational raised €9.6m to track satellites with ground lasers.

Off the map: a sinking lake, and the first beep from space

History and places · sheet 10 of 10
ALake Arendsee, Germanya place
  1. Arendsee is the largest and deepest natural lake in Saxony-Anhalt. It is up to 49 m deep.
  2. It sits on a salt dome. The ground above has caved in again and again, in 822 AD and in 1685.
  3. In 1685 a hill with a mill on it sank into the lake.
  4. This week archaeologists reported worked wood about 10,500 years old, from the Mesolithic.
  5. Divers found a 1.6 m post with felling marks and split boards up to 1.3 m long.
  6. The builders hunted, gathered and fished. They lived about 3,000 years before the region's first farms.

Until now, such buildings in central Germany were known only from stains left by rotted posts. "Whether we are looking at the remains of huts or other, previously completely unknown wooden structures is the subject of ongoing and future research," said the project manager, Marcel Weiß.

BSputnik 1, 69 years ago todaya piece of history
  1. On 4 October 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1 from Baikonur, in today's Kazakhstan.
  2. It was a polished metal ball 58 cm across. It weighed 83.6 kg.
  3. It circled Earth every 96 minutes and beeped on 20.005 and 40.002 MHz. Radio amateurs everywhere could hear it.
  4. Its batteries ran out after about three weeks. It burned up on 4 January 1958.
  5. America answered with ARPA, now DARPA, and NASA, which opened on 1 October 1958.
  6. Its designer, Sergei Korolev, was known in public only as the "chief designer".

This week one rocket carried 130 satellites, and Starship reached orbit. Sputnik's beep carried almost no data; today's limit is getting the data down.
