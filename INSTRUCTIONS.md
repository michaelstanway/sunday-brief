# How to build the Sunday Brief

You are building this week's edition of the Sunday Brief: a 30-minute weekly news read with a 20-question quiz at the bottom. Each edition is its own page at `editions/YYYY-MM-DD.html` (the Sunday's date). `index.html` is the list of all editions, newest first, and is published at https://michaelstanway.github.io/sunday-brief/ (GitHub Pages, `main` branch). The reader opens that same URL every Sunday morning, clicks this week's edition, and enters his quiz score in an app, so the URL, the 20-question quiz and the score-out-of-20 must never change.

## The one question

Before every choice on the page — which story, which panel, which number, which figure, which question — ask: **what does the reader need to understand to make sense of this, and does this element teach it?** The aim is to educate him on the week, not to fill a template. A consistent look is fine; consistent content is not. If an element would be there only because last week had one, leave it out.

These instructions deliberately give no worked examples of stories, figures or questions. Do not copy the shape of a past edition's content. Past editions are a reference for HTML structure only.

## The week you are covering

The edition is dated the Sunday you run on. It covers **the seven days ending the day before** (Sunday to Saturday). Use only facts published by the moment you run. Never use anything you cannot source.

## Steps

1. **Start from last week's edition.** Open the newest file in `editions/`; you will reuse its design (step 6). Never edit or delete past editions.
2. **Collect candidate stories** from these sources. Headlines come from feeds; facts come from free reporting (step 3).
   - The Economist, latest issue: `https://www.economist.com/<section>/rss.xml` for sections `the-world-this-week`, `leaders`, `briefing`, `united-states`, `britain`, `europe`, `china`, `asia`, `the-americas`, `middle-east-and-africa`, `international`, `business`, `finance-and-economics`, `science-and-technology`, `culture`. Fetch with `curl -s -A "Mozilla/5.0 (compatible; RSSReader/1.0)"`. Article pages are paywalled; use only titles and descriptions.
   - Financial Times: `https://www.ft.com/<section>?format=rss` for `world`, `global-economy`, `markets`, `companies`, `technology`, `science`, `climate-capital`, `opinion`, `lex`, `the-big-read`. Feeds only hold the latest 25 items.
   - Money Stuff (Matt Levine): `https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/authors/ARbTQlRLRjE/matthew-s-levine.rss` (headlines only; research the news behind each).
   - Quanta: `https://www.quantamagazine.org/feed/` (full articles are free; read the chosen one in full).
   - Nature news: `https://www.nature.com/nature.rss` (news items have URLs containing `d41586`; ignore research papers). Pages may need `curl` with a cookie jar.
   - Space and Earth observation: `https://payloadspace.com/feed/`, `https://spacenews.com/feed/`, plus searches.
   - Wikipedia Current events, one page per day: `https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Portal:Current_events/2026_September_27&action=raw` (adjust the date).
3. **Research.** Use subagents in parallel (world, markets and money, The Economist's picks, science, space and EO). Every fact comes from a free, reputable source (Reuters, AP, BBC, Al Jazeera, NPR, CNBC, France 24, central banks, journals, company releases). Check every number and quote in the top stories against at least two outlets. If you can't confirm something, leave it out or say "reportedly". Never reconstruct the text of a paywalled article from its headline.
4. **Select: follow The Economist's agenda.** The reader wants the week as The Economist saw it, then filled in from free reporting.
   1. **The backbone is The Economist's issue.** Read this week's "The world this week" (Politics and Business), the leaders, the briefing and the cover story. The cover story and at least three of the leaders each get a sheet unless one is plainly minor. A story The Economist did not cover this week needs a strong reason to get a sheet.
   2. **Then the reader's interests,** at most two sheets between them: space and Earth observation, AI, the UK and California, everyday health. Only when there is real news.
   3. **One wonder piece,** when a genuinely good one exists that week (often Quanta).
   4. **Continuing stories earn a sheet only with a real development.** Open the last two editions. A topic that had a sheet in both gets a sheet this week only if something changed its direction (a war ended or widened, a vote was decided, a market broke a level for a reason worth explaining). Otherwise it gets one line in "Also this week" saying what is new. At least half of this week's sheets must be on topics that had no sheet in either of the last two editions.
   5. **No standing sheets.** There is no fixed markets sheet, no fixed money table, no fixed space sheet. Money gets a sheet when money is the story.
   6. Conflict, crime and disasters together take no more than a third of the page. Drop single-day incidents unless huge; sport gets a line at most.

   Then **rank** the in-depth stories by how much they matter and how much The Economist made of them. The ranking sets the order of the page.
5. **Write** the edition as **8 to 10 story sheets** plus "Also this week" and the quiz. **The page follows the "week in 90 seconds" order:** 6 numbered headlines, in rank order, and sheets 1–6 are those six stories in the same order. Sheets 7 onward are the remaining stories, then the wonder piece. Each sheet's label gives its category and its number (`Europe · sheet 3 of 9`), and the `.toc`, each title block's "Sheet" field and any "see sheet N" pointer use the same numbers.

   **Build each sheet around its idea, not around a panel list.** Start each sheet by writing, for yourself, the one idea a reader should keep from it (a mechanism, a trade-off, a cause, a reason the outcome is uncertain). Then choose the panels that teach that idea, from: What happened (always, first), Why it matters, How it works, The argument (The Economist's view, labelled, plus the strongest counter-view), Key numbers, Timeline, a figure, What to watch, and the Title block (always, last). Most sheets use four or five panels. Two sheets should rarely have the same set.

   **Key numbers, only when they teach.** A Key numbers panel holds two to four numbers, each with a comparison that gives it meaning (a base rate, a previous level, a share of something the reader knows) and a short "so what" in the same row. Never list a number only because it was in the story. If a sheet has no number that changes how the reader understands it, it has no Key numbers panel.

   **What to watch** names the next decision point and what each outcome would mean, not a list of dates.

   **Writing style: about 80% of the way to ASD-STE100 (Simplified Technical English).** One idea per sentence. Most sentences under 20 words, none over 25. Active voice. Simple, common words; the same word for the same thing every time. No more than 6 sentences in a paragraph or list. Quotes are verbatim and attributed. Label The Economist's and Money Stuff's own arguments as theirs. Give dates as weekday names within the week. Explain, don't just report: every sheet says *why*.
5c. **Explain money through the glossary bubbles.** Assume the reader has no finance background: yield, gilt, Treasury, basis point, term premium, the Fed's rate or the yield curve mean nothing to him unless the page explains them. Keep the explanations inside the text, as `<dfn data-def="…">` bubbles, not as separate primer panels or extra table columns. In any sheet or one-liner that leans on finance:
   - Wrap every finance term, instrument and institution on its first use **in that sheet** (not only its first use on the page), including Key numbers labels and What to watch items. A reader who opens one sheet cold must be able to hover through it.
   - Each definition says what the thing is **and why it matters for this story** in one or two plain sentences . A bare dictionary line is not enough.
   - Wrap the phrase that carries the idea, not only a single word .
   - The same applies to any other sheet that leans on specialist ideas (tariffs, sanctions, election systems).

5b. **Write a quiz that makes him think.** The quiz is how the reader keeps what he read. It is out of **20 points, one point per question, exactly 20 questions**, so the score he enters in his app stays comparable week to week.
   - **Ask for judgement, not recall.** Most questions need the reader to reason: why something happened, what follows from it, which explanation fits the evidence, what would change the outcome, how two stories bear on each other. At most 3 ask for a bare fact, and only when the fact is the point.
   - **Apply ideas to new cases.** Several questions set a short new scenario and ask the reader to use a mechanism from the week on it.
   - **Connect.** At least 2 questions need two sheets at once.
   - **Spaced recall.** When past editions exist, 2 questions revisit a story from one to four weeks ago, ideally one that moved this week. Mark them in the explanation ("From 11 October").
   - **Weight toward the top stories,** and test each one at more than one depth. One-liners get no questions.
   - **Mix formats** (all in `assets/quiz.js`, each worth exactly 1 point): `mcq` (one right of four), `multi` (pick all that apply; the point only for the exact set), `order` (put 3–5 items in order: cause to effect, or by size), `estimate` (a number with a slider; the point inside a stated tolerance), `match` (pair 3–4 items with 3–4 others), and `written` (the reader types a short answer, then sees a model answer and marking points and awards himself the point honestly). Use at least four formats each week, no more than 10 `mcq`, and 2 to 3 `written`.
   - **Distractors are real misconceptions,** each plausible to someone who skimmed. No joke options, no "all of the above", the correct answer is not the longest, and correct positions are spread evenly.
   - **The explanation teaches:** the answer, then the reason in one or two sentences.
   - **Never repeat.** Read the last three editions' quizzes and figure problems. No question may reuse a previous question's structure on the same topic (the same mechanism asked the same way). If a topic recurs, ask about what is new.
   - **Order:** easy to hard, roughly following the page.
6. **Build the page.** Create `editions/<this Sunday's date>.html` as a copy of last week's edition, then replace only the content. Keep its structure exactly: the `<head>` (Google Fonts link for Newsreader and IBM Plex Mono, and `../assets/brief.css`; no inline styles), the `.masthead`, the `.strip` (`← All editions`, `Week of …`, `Take the quiz`), the `.hero` block, the `.toc`, the `top` section (the 90 seconds), the `<div class="sheets">` with one `<section class="sheet" id="…">` per story in the order set in step 5, the `rest` section (Also this week), the `quiz` section, the footer, and the quiz data (a `<script type="application/json" id="quiz">` block read by `assets/quiz.js`; see 5b). In the hero, write a real headline for the week as the `<h1>`. It always starts "The week…" and names the week's single biggest story, which is headline 1 and sheet 1, whatever its field: a war, an election, a discovery, a launch, an AI release or a market move. Read the `<h1>` of the last three editions and do not lead on the same topic as either of the last two unless it is clearly the week's biggest story. Then write a one- or two-sentence `.dek` that follows the 90-second order, and update the kicker line (`Sunday Brief · <date> · covers <Sun date> – <Sat date>`) and the dates in the masthead and strip. End every in-depth story with a `<p class="read">Read more: …</p>` line linking the original piece (The Economist, FT, Money Stuff, Quanta or Nature) and the free reporting you used, and end every one-liner with `<span class="src"><a …>Source ↗</a></span>`. All links open in a new tab (`target="_blank" rel="noopener"`) and must be real URLs you fetched or found, never guessed. Story lead-ins like `<strong>This week.</strong>` at the start of a paragraph are styled as small labels; use them for structure. Keep `<meta name="robots" content="noindex, nofollow">`. Never edit `assets/brief.css` unless something is broken. The new edition links its assets as `../assets/brief.css?v=YYYYMMDD` and `../assets/interactive.js?v=YYYYMMDD` (the edition date), so browsers fetch fresh copies; if you change either asset file, also update the `?v=` on every edition so readers get the change. Every edition carries the leaderboard: load `../assets/scores.js?v=…` after `interactive.js`, and put `<div class="board" data-edition="<this Sunday's date>"></div>` directly above the `.score` bar in the quiz section (the script and the scores table do the rest; never write scores into the page). Check that the quiz JSON parses and holds exactly 20 questions.
6b. **Interactive figures, only where doing beats reading.** A figure earns its place when moving something reveals a mechanism the reader would not get from the text: a trade-off, a threshold, a compounding effect, how a system responds. There is no quota. Typically three to six per edition, and none on a sheet where a sentence would do. Components live in `assets/interactive.js` (`timeline`, `predict`, `polls`, `seats`, `seesaw`, `mortgage`, `downlink`, and others); read the file to see what exists. Place each as `<div class="fig" data-fig="NAME" data-config="{...JSON, HTML-escaped...}"></div>` right after the paragraph it explains.
   - **Build new figures for new ideas.** If this week's key idea needs a figure that does not exist, write it in `assets/interactive.js` (a `FIGS.name = function (node, cfg)` using the `frame`, `slider`, `toggles`, `svg` helpers and only CSS variables from `assets/brief.css`), test it, and keep past editions working.
   - **Don't reuse a figure type used in either of the last two editions** unless this week's story is about the same mechanism and the figure is the best way to teach it. A `timeline` is not a teaching figure; use at most one.
   - **`predict`** (guess a striking number before seeing it) only when the surprise itself teaches something: at most two per edition.
   - Every number in a config comes from the edition's sourced text; anything illustrative says so in the figure's note.
   - Glossary terms: wrap the first use of each piece of jargon as `<dfn data-def="one plain-English sentence">term</dfn>` (never inside headings, links or figure configs). Typically 10 to 20 per edition.
6c. **Three levels of depth (progressive disclosure).** Level 1 is the 90-second headlines and each sheet's "What happened" steps. Level 2 is the panels and figures. Level 3 is a `<details class="deeper"><summary>Go deeper: …</summary><div class="deeper-body">…</div></details>` block at the end of every sheet's "What happened" panel (and on any science item with real evidence behind it), holding the fuller account: background, the evidence and numbers, caveats, and attributed quotes, 3 to 5 short paragraphs, each starting with a `<strong>` label. Nothing important is cut from the page; it moves down a level.

   **Everything expands.** Every item the reader sees must open up: each sheet's "What happened" panel, every separate item panel inside the science notebook, space and off-the-map sheets (each gets its own `details.deeper` at the end of its panel), and every one-liner in "Also this week" (put the `details.deeper` inside its `<li>`, after the source link; 2 to 3 paragraphs). The only exceptions are the title blocks, the "week in 90 seconds" list, and pointer lines such as "see sheets 5 and 6". A reader who finds any item interesting must be able to read more about it without leaving the page. Research each expansion like a story: fetch the original paper or release, never pad from the headline.

6d. **A problem for each figure.** Each figure's config carries a `"problem"` that can only be answered by using the figure *and* thinking about the story: `{"type": "choice", "text", "options", "answer", "why", "hint"}`, `{"type": "check", "check": "<name>", …params, "text", "hint", "miss", "why"}` (checks live in `CHECKS` in `assets/interactive.js`), or `{"type": "predict", "text", "why"}` on predict figures. A good problem makes the reader commit to an interpretation, then shows why it holds. It never asks who, when or where, and it never asks something the paragraph above it states outright. The `why` explains the reasoning, not just the answer.

6e. **People cards.** Wrap the first mention of each key person as `<span class="who" data-who="key">Name</span>`, and include a `<script type="application/json" id="people">` block mapping each key to `{"name", "role", "bio", "img", "url"}`, taken from Wikipedia's REST summary API (`https://en.wikipedia.org/api/rest_v1/page/summary/<Title>`: `description`, the first two sentences of `extract`, `thumbnail.source`, `content_urls.desktop.page`). If the summary describes events after the edition date, rewrite the role and bio to what was true on the edition date.

7. **Publish.** Run `python3 build_index.py` to regenerate `index.html` (it reads the kicker line and the first three "week in 90 seconds" headlines from every edition, so keep those structures). Commit the new edition and `index.html` with a message like `Sunday Brief: 4 October 2026`, and push to `main`. Then fetch https://michaelstanway.github.io/sunday-brief/ (Pages takes a minute or two) and confirm the new edition is listed first and its page loads.

## If the network is restricted

Cloud runs may block direct page and feed fetches (curl and WebFetch return 403 or "egress blocked"). If that happens, do not stop: use WebSearch for everything, including finding each source's articles for the week (for example search "quantamagazine.org" with the week's dates, or the publication name plus the topic). Cite only URLs that appeared in search results. If the live-site check in step 7 is blocked, confirm the push succeeded instead and say so in your final report.

## Rules

- Never put anything personal or private in the page beyond the general interests listed above; it is public.
- Never change the site URL, the quiz length (20) or the scoring (one point each, out of 20), and never rename or delete past editions.
- If a source is unreachable, carry on with the rest and say so in the footer.
- Do not touch any other repository.
- Be exact about history: e.g. US troops left Iraq in 2011 and returned in 2014, so the 2026 withdrawal ended a 12-year mission, not a 23-year presence. When a source's framing and the record disagree, follow the record.
